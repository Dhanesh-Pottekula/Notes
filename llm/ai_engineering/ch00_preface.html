CHIP HUYEN · AI ENGINEERING · PREFACE (CHAPTER 0) · PDF p13–24

The preface: why AI engineering is old ideas at a new scale, and how the book walks you through building one app

Chip Huyen opens with a surprise: when ChatGPT arrived, the shock was not that a bigger model was better (people knew that since AlexNet in 2012) but that a modest jump in quality unlocked an explosion of applications and made building them easy for almost anyone. She then argues that AI engineering is built on old techniques (language models from the 1950s, retrieval from search engines, ML best practice) but that scale brings new problems that need new solutions. The book is a framework for adapting foundation models (LLMs and multimodal models) to real applications, focused on fundamentals rather than tools, and it is not a tutorial or a theory book. Its ten chapters follow the life of one AI application: decide whether to build → understand the model → evaluate → improve the instructions (prompts), the context (RAG, agents) and the model (finetuning, data) → make inference cheaper and faster → put it all together with user feedback. The preface ends with O'Reilly housekeeping and a long thank-you list.

01 · PREFACE (OPENING)

What surprised the author about ChatGPT

pdf p13 · footnotes 1–2

When ChatGPT came out, Chip Huyen says she felt disoriented, like many people she worked with. But the surprise was not the model's size or what it could do. The AI community had known for more than ten years that scaling up a model (more parameters, more data, more compute) makes it better.

Her evidence is AlexNet, the 2012 image-recognition paper that started the deep learning boom. Its authors wrote that all their experiments suggested results would improve just by "waiting for faster GPUs and bigger datasets". In other words: the scaling lesson was already on paper in 2012.

  • Footnote 1: one AlexNet author, Ilya Sutskever, went on to co-found OpenAI, and the GPT models are that lesson turned into reality: keep scaling and the model keeps getting better.
  • Footnote 2: even her own small 2017 project, which used a language model to judge translation quality, ended by concluding that what they really needed was a stronger language model. (A modest, funny example of the same lesson: the bottleneck was model quality.)

What actually surprised her was the sheer number of applications the capability boost unlocked. She expected a small gain in model quality metrics to give a modest increase in applications. Instead it gave an explosion of new possibilities.

Two effects happened at once:

  1. Demand went up: the new capabilities made people want many more AI applications.
  2. The entry barrier went down: it became very easy to start building. You can even build an AI application without writing any code.

Together, these turned AI from a specialised discipline (something only ML teams with data and training infrastructure could do) into a development tool that everyone can use.

Picture it

Think of UPI. For years, digital payments existed (cards, net banking) and each small improvement added a few more users. Then UPI crossed a line: instant, free, works with a QR code stuck on a chai stall. Suddenly every vegetable seller, auto driver and kirana shop could accept digital money, and new uses (splitting bills, paying rent, tipping) appeared that nobody planned. The technology improved a bit; the number of uses exploded, and the barrier for a shopkeeper fell to "print a QR code". Where it breaks: UPI's jump was mostly about cost and convenience set by policy; ChatGPT's jump was about model capability crossing a usefulness threshold for language tasks.

What's happening behind it

Many tasks are all-or-nothing in usefulness. A model that writes a correct email 60% of the time is useless for that job; at 95% it is a product. So a smooth rise in model quality turns into a step change in the number of tasks that become worth automating. On top of that, the model now comes as an API (application programming interface) that takes plain-English instructions, so the cost of trying an idea dropped from "collect data and train a model" to "write a prompt". Chapter 1 calls this new discipline AI engineering.

Interview angle

"What changed with foundation models from an engineering point of view?" Say: "Before, every ML feature needed its own labelled data and a trained model, so only ML teams could ship them. Now one general model, behind an API, can do many tasks from instructions, so product engineers can build AI features directly. The work moved from training models to adapting and evaluating them: prompts, context, evaluation, latency and cost." This fits work like realtime voice agents, where the hard part is orchestration and latency, not training.

02 · PREFACE (OPENING)

Old techniques, new scale: why AI engineering is both familiar and new

pdf p13–14

Even though AI adoption feels new, it rests on techniques that have existed for a long time. The book gives three examples:

  1. Language modelling: papers about language models appeared as early as the 1950s.
  2. RAG (retrieval-augmented generation): fetching relevant information and giving it to the model. It is built on the retrieval technology that has powered search engines and recommender systems since long before anyone said "RAG".
  3. Deployment best practices from traditional ML still apply: systematic experimentation, rigorous evaluation, and relentless optimisation for faster and cheaper models. These remain the best practices for applications built on foundation models.

Then the warning: because many AI engineering techniques feel familiar and easy, people can be misled into thinking there is nothing new in AI engineering. That is wrong. Many principles stay the same, but the scale and improved capabilities of AI models create opportunities and challenges that need new solutions.

So the book's scope is the end-to-end process of adapting foundation models to real-world problems, covering both tried-and-true techniques from other engineering fields and techniques that emerged with foundation models.

Picture it

Indian Railways' reservation system: the idea (a ledger of seats per train per date) is as old as paper registers at the station. But when Tatkal opens at 10 a.m. and lakhs of people book in the same minute, the old idea needs new solutions: queues, caching, captchas, load shedding. AI engineering is the same: retrieval, evaluation and experimentation are old registers; the scale and open-ended outputs of foundation models are the Tatkal rush. Where it breaks: the railway's load problem is only about volume; with foundation models the kind of output also changed (free text instead of a label), which is what makes evaluation so much harder (Ch.3).

What's happening behind it

Old ML models gave closed outputs (a class, a number), so you could check them against a label. Foundation models give open-ended outputs (an essay, a plan, code) and can do many tasks, so the same practice ("evaluate rigorously") needs new tools: AI judges, comparative evaluation, and so on. Bigger models also cost more per request and are slower, so "optimise for faster and cheaper" becomes a whole chapter (Ch.9) instead of a footnote.

Interview angle

"Is RAG just search?" A good answer: "The retrieval half is classic information retrieval: term-based search like BM25 and embedding-based vector search, the same tools used in search and recommendations. What is new is that the consumer of the results is a model with a limited context window, so you care about chunking, how many results fit, ordering, and whether the model actually uses the context. So it's old retrieval plus new context-construction problems."

Beyond those questions, the book promises to help you find your way through the overwhelming AI landscape: the types of models, the evaluation benchmarks, and the seemingly endless use cases and application patterns.

How the content is backed:

  • It is illustrated with case studies, many of which the author worked on herself.
  • It is supported by ample references and was reviewed extensively by experts from many backgrounds.
  • The book took two years to write, but draws on her experience with language models and ML systems over the last decade.

Like her earlier O'Reilly book, Designing Machine Learning Systems (DMLS), this one focuses on the fundamentals of AI engineering rather than any specific tool or API. Her reason: tools go out of date quickly; fundamentals should last longer. Footnote 3 gives the lesson behind this: teaching a course on how to use TensorFlow in 2017 showed her, painfully, how fast tools and tutorials become outdated.

Picture it

A good cooking teacher does not teach "how to use the 2024 model of a particular pressure cooker". She teaches why pressure cooks dal faster, how much water rice needs, when to add salt. Then any cooker (new brand, new buttons) is easy. The book is the cooking teacher; LangChain or a vector database's API is the cooker's button layout. Where it breaks: in cooking the fundamentals almost never change; in AI some "fundamentals" (like how long a context window is) do shift, which is why plate 06 explains how she decided what counts as lasting.

What's happening behind it

"Adapting" a foundation model is the core job because you almost never train one yourself. You take a general model and make it good at your task by changing what goes into it (prompt, context) or changing the model a little (finetuning). Each adaptation has costs (latency, money, data, engineering time), so the book is organised as trade-offs plus questions, not as one right recipe.

Interview angle

"How would you decide between prompting, RAG and finetuning?" Say: "I'd start with prompting and a solid evaluation set, because it's the cheapest to iterate. If failures come from missing or stale information, I add context with RAG. If failures are about behaviour or format that prompting can't fix reliably, or I need a smaller, cheaper model, I consider finetuning. Every step is decided by evaluation, not by taste." The preface's question list is basically this interview question split into parts.

Picture it

DMLS is like learning to cook from raw ingredients: you grind your own masala (feature engineering), test recipes again and again (training). AIE is like running a kitchen with a superb ready-made base gravy: your skill is in instructions to the cook (prompts), what you add at the end (context) and small tweaks to the base (parameter-efficient finetuning). A restaurant uses both. Where it breaks: you can taste a gravy instantly; judging a foundation model's open-ended answers needs a whole evaluation pipeline.

What's happening behind it

In traditional ML the model is yours: you collect labelled data and train it, so most effort is data and training. With foundation models someone else already spent the training budget, so the model is fixed or nearly fixed and your effort moves to the inputs (prompt and context) and to light-touch weight changes. That shift is why the work list changes between the two books.

Worked example (modelled scenario)

A food-delivery app: predicting delivery time from distance, traffic and restaurant load is a tabular, traditional ML problem (DMLS). Answering "where is my order and can I change the address?" in chat is a foundation model problem (AIE): prompt, retrieve the order status as context, call a tool. The same product needs both, which is the sidebar's last point.

Interview angle

"What's the difference between ML engineering and AI engineering?" Say: "ML engineering usually means training your own models on your own labelled data, with feature engineering. AI engineering means building on a pretrained foundation model: the focus shifts to prompts, context construction like RAG, evaluating open-ended outputs, and sometimes parameter-efficient finetuning, plus serving cost and latency. Real systems mix both, for example a classic ranking model feeding a generative model."

06 · WHAT THIS BOOK IS ABOUT (CONTINUED)

How she decided what will last: three criteria

pdf p16

Focusing on fundamentals only works if you can tell what will last, and that is often hard. She used three criteria:

  1. Is the problem fundamental? For each problem, she asked whether it comes from the fundamental limitations of how AI works or whether it will go away with better models. If it is fundamental, the book analyses its challenges and the solutions for each challenge. She likes a start-simple approach: for many problems the book begins with the simplest solution and moves to more complex solutions as new challenges appear.
  2. Ask the experts. She consulted a large network of researchers and engineers (she calls them smarter than herself) about what they see as the most important problems and solutions.
  3. Lindy's Law, used occasionally: the future life expectancy of a technology is proportional to its current age. If something has been around a long time, assume it will stay around a while longer.

Exception: she sometimes included a concept she believes is temporary, either because it is immediately useful for some application developers, or because it shows an interesting problem-solving approach.

Picture it

Lindy's Law is why you would bet that the pressure cooker and the tiffin box will still be in Indian kitchens in 30 years, but not this year's viral kitchen gadget. They have survived decades already; the gadget has survived months. Where it breaks: it is a rule of thumb for things that do not age like living beings. A technology can be killed suddenly by a better replacement (film cameras), so she uses it only "occasionally", as one signal among three.

What's happening behind it

Criterion 1 is the strongest filter. A model's knowledge cut-off, the fact that it samples outputs probabilistically, the cost of running large models, and the difficulty of judging open-ended answers follow from how these models work, so better models change the details, not the existence of the problem. Something like "this model can't count letters well" might vanish next release. Spending pages on the first kind pays off; spending pages on the second ages badly. "Start simple" fits the same idea: the simple solution usually survives, and you only add complexity when a real challenge forces it.

Interview angle

"How do you avoid over-engineering an LLM feature?" Say: "Start with the simplest thing that could work, like a single prompt with a good eval set, then add complexity, like retrieval, tool calls or finetuning, only when evaluation shows a specific failure that the simpler version can't fix. I also ask whether a problem is fundamental or will disappear with the next model, so I don't build heavy workarounds for temporary weaknesses."

07 · WHAT THIS BOOK IS NOT

Not a tutorial, not a theory book, and what background helps

pdf p16–17

Not a tutorial. The book names specific tools and includes pseudocode snippets to explain some concepts, but it does not teach you how to use any tool. It gives a framework for selecting tools, with many discussions of trade-offs between solutions and the questions to ask when evaluating one. When you want to use a particular tool, tutorials are easy to find online, and AI chatbots are good at helping you get started with popular tools.

Not an ML theory book. It does not explain what a neural network is or how to build and train a model from scratch. It does explain theory that is directly relevant to the discussion, but it is a practical book about building successful AI applications for real-world problems.

But background helps. You can build foundation-model applications without ML expertise, and you can read the book without any ML background. Still, a basic understanding of ML and statistics helps you build better applications and "save you from unnecessary suffering". You will be more effective if you know these concepts:

Picture it

The book is like a guide to running a restaurant, not a cooking-school course and not a manual for one brand of oven. What's happening behind it

Why sampling and distributions matter even if you never train a model: an LLM's output is sampled from a probability distribution, so the same prompt can give different answers. That one fact explains inconsistency, why temperature settings matter, and why you need many test cases instead of one. Metrics like precision/recall and cosine similarity are the vocabulary of evaluation and retrieval, which take up a large part of the book.

Worked example: precision and recall

A RAG retriever returns 10 chunks for a question; 6 are relevant. There are 8 relevant chunks in the whole store. Precision = 6/10 = 0.6. Recall = 6/8 = 0.75. F1 = 2·0.6·0.75/(0.6+0.75) ≈ 0.67. (Modelled numbers; Chapter 6 uses exactly these ideas to evaluate retrieval.)

Interview angle

"Why does the same prompt give different answers, and how do you handle it?" Say: "The model outputs a probability distribution over next tokens and the decoder samples from it, so with temperature above zero the output varies. For deterministic-looking behaviour you lower temperature or use greedy decoding, constrain the output format, and you evaluate over many samples instead of trusting one run." This is Chapter 2 material, and the preface lists it as a useful prerequisite.

08 · WHO THIS BOOK IS FOR

Who should read it

pdf p17

It is for anyone who wants to use foundation models to solve real-world problems. It is a technical book aimed at technical roles: AI engineers, ML engineers, data scientists, engineering managers and technical product managers.

The book is for you if one of these scenarios fits:

  1. You are building or optimising an AI application, from scratch or trying to move beyond the demo into production. You may be facing hallucinations, security, latency or costs and need targeted fixes.
  2. You want to make your team's AI development process more systematic, faster and more reliable.
  3. You want to understand how your organisation can use foundation models to improve the business's bottom line, and how to build a team to do it.

You can also benefit if you are one of these:

  • Tool developers looking for underserved areas in AI engineering to position products.
  • Researchers who want to understand AI use cases better.
  • Job candidates who want clarity on the skills needed for an AI engineer career.
  • Anyone who wants to understand AI's capabilities and limitations and how it might affect different roles.

A heads-up: she likes getting to the bottom of things, so some sections go deeper technically. Many early readers liked the detail, but it is not for everyone; she will warn you before things get too technical, and you can skip ahead.

09 · NAVIGATING THIS BOOK (1 OF 3)

The book follows the life of one AI application: Chapters 1–4

pdf p18

The book is ordered to follow the typical process of developing an AI application, and each chapter fits one stage. It is modular: skip any section you already know or do not need.

Step through: the development process, chapter by chapter

Chapter 1: should you build it? Before deciding to build an AI application you need to understand what the process involves and answer questions like: Is this application necessary? Is AI needed? Do I have to build it myself? Chapter 1 helps answer these, and covers a range of successful use cases to show what foundation models can do.

Chapter 2: how a foundation model works. You do not need an ML background, but knowing what happens under the hood helps you get the most out of a model. Chapter 2 looks at how a foundation model is made and at the design decisions that strongly affect downstream applications:

  • its training data recipe,
  • its model architecture and scale,
  • how it is trained to align with human preference.

It then explains how a model generates a response, which helps explain seemingly baffling behaviour like inconsistency and hallucinations. Changing the model's generation setting (for example sampling parameters) is often a cheap and easy way to boost performance significantly.

Chapters 3 and 4: evaluation. Once you commit to building with foundation models, evaluation becomes part of every step. She calls it one of the hardest, maybe the hardest, challenge of AI engineering. Two whole chapters cover different evaluation methods and how to use them to build a reliable, systematic evaluation pipeline for your application.

Try it: Chapter 1's three gate questions (modelled scenarios)

Pick an idea, then read how the three questions from the preface apply. The ideas and verdicts are my illustrations.

Picture it

Building an AI app is like opening a chai stall. First: should you open it at all, here, and do you need a fancy machine or will a stove do (Ch.1)? Then: know your ingredients, how tea leaves and milk behave when boiled (Ch.2). And from day one, taste every batch and ask customers (Ch.3–4). Where it breaks: tasting chai is quick and one person can judge it; judging an AI's answers often needs other AI models as judges, with their own biases.

What's happening behind it

Why put evaluation so early, before prompts and RAG? Because every later technique is a change you make to improve quality, and you cannot know if a change helped without a way to measure. Building the evaluation pipeline first turns prompt tweaks, retrieval changes and model swaps into measurable experiments instead of guesses. And generation settings come in Chapter 2 because they are the cheapest lever of all: no new data, no new model, just different sampling.

Interview angle

"You have a week to ship an LLM feature. What do you do first?" Say: "Decide if AI is needed and what 'good' means, then write a small evaluation set of real inputs with expected behaviour, before tuning anything. Then the simplest prompt, measure, iterate. Without evals, every change is a vibe check." This mirrors the book's order: Ch.1 → Ch.3–4 → Ch.5.

The three levers of response quality, and Chapters 5–6

pdf p18–19

This is the most useful idea in the preface. For a given query, apart from the model's generation setting, the quality of the response depends on three things:

1 · Instructionshow the model should behave → Chapter 5 (prompt engineering)
2 · Contextthe information the model can use to answer → Chapter 6 (RAG and agents)
3 · The model itselfits weights → Chapter 7 (finetuning), with Chapter 8 on the data

The next chapters optimise each of these to improve the model's performance for an application.

Chapter 5: prompt engineering. It starts with what a prompt is, why prompt engineering works, and best practices. Then it covers how bad actors can exploit your application with prompt attacks, and how to defend against them.

Chapter 6: context. It explains why context is important for accurate responses, and zooms into two major application patterns for context construction:

RAG patternBetter understood, and has proven to work well in production.

Pick a failure. The lever lights up above with a reason. The failures and diagnoses are my examples built on the preface's three-lever model.

Picture it

Asking a new intern at a CA firm to file a client's GST return. The instructions are what you tell him ("use the March invoices, flag anything above ₹1 lakh"). The context is the client's folder you hand him. The model is the intern himself: his training and skill. If the return is wrong, you ask: were my instructions unclear, was the folder missing invoices, or does he just not know GST yet? Each needs a different fix: rewrite instructions, give the folder, or send him on a course (finetuning). Where it breaks: the intern learns from every task; an LLM does not remember between calls unless you put the memory back into the context.

What's happening behind it

A model's output is a function of its weights and its input. The input is the instructions plus the context plus the query. So you can only change the output by changing the input (Ch.5, Ch.6), changing the weights (Ch.7, with data from Ch.8), or changing how tokens are picked from the output distribution (generation setting, Ch.2). The three levers are not a style choice; they are the only knobs there are. They also rise in cost: editing a prompt is minutes, building retrieval is days, finetuning needs data and GPUs.

Interview angle

"Your RAG chatbot gives wrong answers. How do you debug it?" Say: "I split by lever. First check context: was the right document retrieved and was it in the prompt? If not, it's a retrieval problem (chunking, embeddings, search). If the right context was there and the answer still ignored it, it's instructions or the model: tighten the prompt, then consider a stronger model or finetuning. Logging the full assembled prompt per request is what makes this split possible." This is exactly how you'd debug a RAG memory layer in a voice agent.

Chapters 7–10: the model, the data, inference, and the whole system

Chapter 7: finetuning. It is about adapting a model to an application by changing the model itself. Because foundation models are so big, native finetuning (updating all weights) is memory-intensive, so many techniques exist to let you finetune better models with less memory. The chapter covers different finetuning approaches, plus a more experimental approach: model merging (combining several models into one). It has a more technical section on how to calculate a model's memory footprint.

Chapter 8: data. Thanks to the many finetuning frameworks, finetuning itself is often straightforward. Getting the data is hard. Many topics there matter beyond finetuning, including what data quality means and how to evaluate your data's quality.

Picture it

Continuing the CA-firm intern: Chapter 7 is sending him on a GST course (changing the person); Chapter 8 is finding good practice cases for that course, which is the hard part; Chapter 9 is making him work faster and cheaper (better tools, a smaller desk, batching all the filings for one day); Chapter 10 is designing the whole office, plus a feedback form clients will actually fill in. Where it breaks: retraining an intern does not cost GPU memory; finetuning a model of billions of weights does, which is why memory-saving methods get so much space.

What's happening behind it

Why is data harder than finetuning? Finetuning code is generic and now packaged in frameworks, but data is specific to your task: it must be relevant, correct, diverse and formatted the way the model will be used. Nobody can download it for you. And why does inference optimisation depend on API vs self-hosting? Because the speed and cost of serving tokens is decided by who runs the GPUs: with an API, the provider has already tuned batching, caching and hardware; when you host, all of that is yours.

Interview angle

"Would you self-host an open model or use an API?" Say: "API first for speed of iteration and because the provider handles inference optimisation. Self-host when cost at volume, latency control, data privacy or customisation (like a finetuned model) justify the operational work, and then I own batching, quantisation, KV caching and autoscaling, which is Chapter 9's material." For an LLM gateway, this choice is often per route.

Summary

pdf p13–23
  • The ChatGPT surprise was not that scale helps (known since AlexNet, 2012) but that a modest quality gain caused an explosion of applications and a much lower entry barrier, turning AI into a tool everyone can use.
  • AI engineering rests on old techniques (language models since the 1950s, retrieval from search/recommenders, ML best practices: experiment, evaluate, optimise), but scale and capability create new challenges that need new solutions.
  • The book is a framework for adapting foundation models (LLMs and LMMs) to applications: solutions plus the questions to choose between them. It is based on case studies, 100+ interviews, references and expert review.
  • It focuses on fundamentals, not tools, choosing lasting topics with three tests: fundamental limit vs fixed by better models (starting simple), expert opinion, and Lindy's Law. A few temporary but useful topics are included anyway.
  • It is a companion to DMLS: DMLS for traditional ML (tabular data, features, training), AIE for foundation models (prompts, context, parameter-efficient finetuning). Real systems need both.
  • It is not a tutorial and not an ML theory book; ML and statistics basics (sampling, gradient descent, transformers, precision/recall, cross entropy…) help but are not required.
  • It is for technical roles building or improving AI applications, teams making the process systematic, leaders planning AI strategy, plus tool builders, researchers, job seekers and the curious.
  • The chapters follow one application's life: Ch.1 should I build? → Ch.2 how models work → Ch.3–4 evaluation → the three levers: Ch.5 instructions, Ch.6 context, Ch.7 the model (with Ch.8 data) → Ch.9 inference → Ch.10 architecture and user feedback.

Check yourself

13 questions
1. What did the author find surprising about ChatGPT, and what did not surprise her?

Not its size or capability: scaling was known to help since at least AlexNet (2012). What surprised her was the explosion in the number of applications a modest quality gain unlocked, and how low the entry barrier became.

2. What did the AlexNet authors predict, and how does footnote 1 connect it to GPT?

That results would improve just by waiting for faster GPUs and bigger datasets. Ilya Sutskever, an AlexNet author, co-founded OpenAI, whose GPT models turned that scaling lesson into reality.

3. Name three "old" techniques that AI engineering builds on.

Language modelling (papers from the 1950s); retrieval from search and recommender systems (the base of RAG); traditional ML deployment best practices: systematic experimentation, rigorous evaluation, optimising for faster and cheaper models.

4. Why is "there's nothing new in AI engineering" wrong?

The principles carry over, but the scale and capabilities of foundation models create new opportunities and challenges (open-ended outputs, cost, latency, security) that need new solutions.

5. What are the three criteria she used to decide what will last?

(1) Is the problem a fundamental limitation of how AI works, or will better models remove it? (2) What do expert researchers and engineers think matters most? (3) Occasionally, Lindy's Law: older technologies are expected to last longer.

6. What if a technology is only two months old: what would Lindy's Law predict, and why did she still sometimes include temporary concepts?

A short remaining life (roughly proportional to its age). She includes some temporary concepts when they are immediately useful to developers or show an interesting problem-solving approach.

7. How do DMLS and AIE differ in the work they describe?

DMLS: traditional ML, so more tabular data annotation, feature engineering and model training. AIE: foundation models, so more prompt engineering, context construction and parameter-efficient finetuning. Both are self-contained; real systems need both.

8. What two things is the book explicitly not?

Not a tutorial (it gives a framework for choosing tools, with trade-offs and questions) and not an ML theory book (no neural networks or training from scratch, only theory directly relevant to building apps).

9. Besides the generation setting, what three things decide response quality, and which chapters cover them?

Instructions (Ch.5 prompt engineering), context (Ch.6 RAG and agents), and the model itself (Ch.7 finetuning, with Ch.8 on data).

10. Why does the book compare RAG and agentic patterns the way it does?

RAG is better understood and proven in production; the agentic pattern promises much more power but is more complex and still being explored.

11. You call a hosted model API. How much of Chapter 9 do you need to implement?

Little: the API provider will likely handle inference optimisation. If you self-host an open-source or in-house model, you need to implement many of Chapter 9's techniques yourself.

12. Why is finetuning often easy but finetuning projects still hard?

Many frameworks make the finetuning process straightforward; getting good data (acquire, annotate, synthesise, process, judge quality) is the hard part (Ch.8). Also native finetuning of big models is memory-hungry, hence memory-efficient methods (Ch.7).

13. Interview: "Your LLM chatbot answers confidently but wrongly about company policy. Walk me through your fix."

Diagnose by lever with an eval set: check whether the right policy document was retrieved and included (context, Ch.6); if it was, tighten instructions to answer only from context and say "I don't know" otherwise (Ch.5); if it still fails, try a stronger model or finetuning (Ch.7). Lower temperature can reduce variability (Ch.2). Measure each change (Ch.3–4) and add user feedback signals (Ch.10).
