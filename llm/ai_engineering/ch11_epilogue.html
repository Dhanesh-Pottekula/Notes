03 · THE MAIN POINT

The hard part is asking the right questions, not finding answers

pdf p519

This is the heart of the epilogue. The author says the hardest part of technical writing is not finding correct answers; it is asking the right questions. Writing the book pushed her to ask many questions, and those questions led her to discoveries that were both fun and useful. She hopes the book did the same for you: that it left you with interesting questions of your own.

Why this matters for AI engineering: foundation models are probabilistic (Chapter 2), open-ended and hard to evaluate (Chapter 3). There is rarely one correct answer to look up. Progress comes from asking a sequence of sharper questions about your own application. The book itself is built this way: Chapter 1 asks "should you even build this?", Chapter 7 asks "should you even finetune?", Chapter 10 asks "what happens if users' feedback is wrong?".

Worked example · climbing the question ladder (step-through)

A vague question becomes a useful one step by step. The scenario is modelled (a support chatbot for a food-delivery app); each step points to the chapter that taught you to ask it.

Picture it

A good doctor does not start with the medicine; they start with questions: since when, where does it hurt, any fever? The right questions shrink the space of answers until the answer is obvious. Where it breaks: a doctor has textbooks of known diseases; in AI engineering many problems are new, so sometimes your question has no known answer yet and you must run an experiment (an eval) to get one.

What's happening behind it

Answers are cheap now: a foundation model can produce a fluent answer to almost anything. What is scarce is knowing which answer you need and how to check it. That is why the book spends two chapters on evaluation: an evaluation is a question made precise enough that a machine can answer it.

Interview angle

System design rounds reward this directly. Before drawing boxes, ask: who are the users, what does a good answer look like, what is the latency budget, what does one wrong answer cost, how will we measure it? Saying "Before I design, let me pin down the evaluation criteria and the latency and cost budget" signals seniority. In your own work (realtime voice agents), the latency budget question alone decides model size, streaming and caching choices.

04 · OUTLOOK

Applications will keep multiplying; systematic methods make it easier

pdf p519

The author notes that many impressive applications are already built on foundation models (large models trained on huge data that can be adapted to many tasks, from Chapter 1). She has no doubt that their number will grow exponentially. Her claim is that more systematic approaches to AI engineering, such as the ones in the book, will make development easier, and that easier development will in turn enable even more applications.

"Systematic" is the key word. Chapter 2's summary (pdf p136) frames the whole book this way: models are probabilistic, so AI engineering can't be fully deterministic, but it can be systematic, and the first step is a solid evaluation pipeline.

Ad-hoc vs systematic: how fast a team improves (modelled)

Modelled, not from the book. Ad-hoc team: each week's change helps or hurts at random, because nobody measures it, so bad changes ship. Systematic team: an eval set catches regressions, so only changes that improve the score ship. The point is the shape: measurement turns random walks into steady climbs.

Picture it

UPI made payments systematic: one standard way to send money, so thousands of apps could be built on top quickly. A shared, systematic way to build AI apps (evaluate, then prompt, then retrieve, then finetune, then serve) does the same for AI products. Where it breaks: UPI is one fixed protocol run by one body; AI engineering "systems" are practices that each team adapts, and they keep changing as models change.

What's happening behind it

The feedback loop: easier tools → more teams can build → more applications → more lessons and tools. Chapter 1 explains why the barrier fell in the first place: with model APIs, you can build the product first and invest in data and models only once the product shows promise (the workflow in Figure 1-16, pdf p71).

Interview angle

"How do you make an LLM feature reliable when the model is non-deterministic?" Answer: "You can't make it deterministic, but you can make the process systematic: a versioned eval set, automatic checks plus AI-judge or human checks, experiment tracking of prompts and sampling settings, and monitoring in production. Every change has to beat the current score before it ships."

Read the book as an order of operations. First understand the model (Ch.1–2). Then build the measuring stick (Ch.3–4). Ch.1 · Introduction to Building AI Applications with Foundation Models

productdesignpdf p25–72
  • From language models to LLMs (self-supervision) to foundation models (more modalities) to AI engineering.
  • Use cases (consumer and enterprise); whether to build; the AI engineering stack and how it differs from ML engineering.
  • Product-first workflow: build the product with a model API, invest in data and models only once it shows promise.

Likely questions: What's the difference between ML engineering and AI engineering? Should this feature use an LLM at all? Build vs buy?

Say it: "ML engineering usually starts with data and a trained model; AI engineering usually starts from a ready foundation model and focuses on adapting and evaluating it, so the work moves closer to product. I'd first check whether the feature needs AI, what a wrong answer costs, and prototype with an API before investing in finetuning."

Confidence:

Ch.2 · Understanding Foundation Models

fundamentalspdf p73–136
  • Training data (languages, domains); transformer architecture and its limits; model size: parameters, training tokens, FLOPs; scaling law.
  • Post-training = supervised finetuning + preference finetuning.
  • Sampling makes models probabilistic: good for creativity, cause of inconsistency and hallucination.

Likely questions: What do temperature and top-p do? Why do LLMs hallucinate? What is RLHF / preference finetuning for? What does the scaling law tell you?

Say it: "The model outputs a probability for every next token; sampling picks one. Temperature reshapes the distribution (low = safer, repetitive; high = varied, riskier), top-p keeps only the smallest set of tokens covering p of the probability. Pre-training gives knowledge, SFT teaches the conversation format, preference finetuning aligns answers with what people prefer."

Confidence:

Ch.3 · Evaluation Methodology

fundamentalsdesignpdf p137–182
  • Why foundation models are harder to evaluate than classic ML models.
  • Language modeling metrics: cross entropy, perplexity.
  • Exact evaluation (functional correctness, similarity scores) vs subjective AI-as-a-judge; comparative evaluation and preference models.

Likely questions: How do you evaluate a free-text answer? Is LLM-as-a-judge reliable? What is perplexity?

Say it: "Prefer exact checks where possible: does the code run, does the JSON parse, does it match a reference. For open-ended quality, use an AI judge with a clear rubric, but its scores depend on the judge model and prompt, so pin and version them, and back them with exact checks or human review."

Confidence:

Ch.4 · Evaluate AI Systems

designproductpdf p183–234
  • Evaluation criteria: domain capability, generation quality (factual consistency, safety), plus fluency, coherence, faithfulness.
  • Model selection; host a model vs use an API, compared along axes such as data privacy, data lineage, performance, functionality, control, cost.
  • Public benchmarks weed out bad models but are likely contaminated; build your own evaluation pipeline and iterate on it with experiment tracking.

Likely questions: How would you choose a model for this product? API or self-host? How do you build an eval pipeline?

Say it: "Leaderboards only shortlist. I build a private leaderboard: an eval set from real user tasks, criteria with rubrics, then compare shortlisted models on quality, latency and cost. API vs self-host comes down to privacy, control and cost at our volume. I log the eval data, rubric, prompts and judge settings so results stay comparable."

Confidence:

Ch.5 · Prompt Engineering

designfundamentalspdf p235–276
  • Anatomy of a prompt; in-context learning; best practices (clear instructions, examples, context, step-by-step thinking).
  • Defensive prompt engineering: prompt attacks and defences; guardrails on inputs and outputs; spotting abuse by usage patterns.

Likely questions: What is prompt injection and how do you defend against it? Zero-shot vs few-shot? When does chain-of-thought help?

Say it: "Prompt injection is untrusted text making the model follow instructions it shouldn't. No defence is foolproof, so layer them: separate system and user content, input filters for known attack patterns, output guardrails for PII and toxicity, least-privilege tools, and watch usage patterns for someone probing."

Confidence:

Ch.6 · RAG and Agents

designbackendpdf p277–330
  • RAG (retrieval-augmented generation): retrieve from external memory, then generate. Quality depends on the retriever.
  • Term-based retrievers (Elasticsearch, BM25) are light, strong baselines; embedding-based retrievers cost more compute and can do better; vector search powers them.
  • Agents = environment + tools; the model plans; reflection and memory help; more tools = more power and more risk.
  • Memory systems for information beyond the context window.

Likely questions: Design a RAG chatbot over company docs. BM25 or embeddings? How do you evaluate retrieval? How do you stop an agent doing damage?

Say it: "Start with a term-based baseline like BM25, then add embedding retrieval and combine them; measure retrieval separately from generation. For agents I give the fewest tools needed, require confirmation for write actions, cap steps, and log every tool call, because the more automated an agent is, the worse its failures."

Confidence:

Ch.7 · Finetuning

fundamentalsdesignpdf p331–386
  • Reasons to finetune and not to; finetuning vs RAG.
  • Memory bottlenecks: more trainable parameters = more memory. PEFT reduces trainable parameters; quantized training reduces bits per value.
  • LoRA: parameter- and data-efficient, modular (easy to serve and combine). Model merging. Prompt loss weight (default often 10%).

Likely questions: RAG or finetuning? How does LoRA work and why is it cheap? How much memory does finetuning need?

Say it: "RAG for missing or changing facts; finetuning for form: style, format, a narrow skill. Full finetuning updates every weight and needs huge memory. LoRA freezes the base and trains small low-rank matrices, so it needs far less memory and you can serve many adapters on one base model."

Confidence:

Ch.8 · Dataset Engineering

fundamentalsproductpdf p387–428
  • Start from the behaviours you want, then design data that shows them.
  • Three criteria in every phase: quality, coverage, quantity. Small high-quality data can beat large noisy data; diversity matters.
  • Synthetic data (with a deep dive on instruction data) must be verified like real data. Annotation guidelines are hard to automate.

Likely questions: How would you build a finetuning dataset? Can you use synthetic data? How do you check data quality?

Say it: "I list the behaviours first, then collect or synthesise examples covering them, and check quality, coverage and quantity. Synthetic data from a strong model is fine if I can verify it: automatic checks plus a human-reviewed sample. A small clean set usually beats a big noisy one."

Confidence:

Ch.9 · Inference Optimization

backenddesignpdf p429–472
  • Metrics: latency split into TTFT (time to first token, from prefilling) and TPOT (time per output token, from decoding); throughput (tied to cost); utilization. Latency and throughput trade off.
  • AI hardware overview. Model-level: quantization, distillation, attention/KV-cache optimisation, faster autoregressive decoding.
  • Service-level: batching, parallelism (replica, tensor, pipeline, context, sequence), prefill/decode decoupling, prompt caching.
  • Most impactful in general: quantization, tensor parallelism, replica parallelism, attention optimisation.

Likely questions: Our LLM endpoint is slow; what do you look at? What's TTFT vs TPOT? What is the KV cache? How does batching affect latency?

Say it: "First split latency into TTFT and TPOT. Long prompts hurt TTFT, so prompt caching and shorter context help; long answers hurt TPOT, so quantization or a smaller model help. Batching raises throughput and lowers cost but can raise per-request latency. For strict latency I'd scale replicas rather than use pipeline parallelism."

Confidence:

Ch.10 · AI Engineering Architecture and User Feedback

designbackendproductpdf p473–518
  • A common architecture built step by step: add context, guardrails, a model router and gateway, caches, agent patterns.
  • Component boundaries are fluid (e.g. guardrails can live in the inference service, the gateway, or alone); each component adds capability and failure modes.
  • Monitoring and observability for new AI failure modes.
  • Conversational user feedback for analytics, product improvement and the data flywheel; dangers: degenerate feedback loops, sycophancy.

Likely questions: Draw the architecture of a production LLM app. What does a model gateway do? What do you monitor? How do you use user feedback safely?

Say it: "Request comes in → input guardrails → context construction (RAG, tools) → router picks a model through a gateway that handles keys, fallbacks, rate limits and cost tracking → output guardrails → response, with caches in front and traces everywhere. Feedback goes to analytics and eval sets, but I check it for bias before training on it, or the model learns to please instead of to be right."

Check yourself

12 questions
1. What four numbers does the epilogue give to describe the book's size?

More than 150,000 words, 160 illustrations, 250 footnotes, and 975 reference links.

2. According to the author, what is the hardest part of technical writing?

Asking the right questions, not finding the correct answers. Writing the book made her ask many questions that led to fun and useful discoveries.

3. What does she predict about applications built on foundation models, and what will help?

Their number will grow exponentially. More systematic approaches to AI engineering, like the book's, will make development easier and so enable even more applications.

4. Where does the epilogue send you for more resources?

The book's GitHub repository, github.com/chiphuyen/aie-book.

5. How does the epilogue frame AI engineering's challenges?

Not all are fun, but all are opportunities for growth and impact.

6. Why can AI engineering be "systematic" but not "deterministic"?

Sampling makes model outputs probabilistic (Ch.2), so the same input can give different outputs. You can't remove that, but you can build a repeatable process around it, starting with an evaluation pipeline that detects failures and unexpected changes.

7. What would happen if a team skipped evaluation to ship faster?

Changes would be judged by gut feeling, so regressions would ship unnoticed and progress would wander. Ch.4 calls skipping evaluation (for example to cut latency) a risky bet; without a reliable pipeline you can't reduce risk, find improvements, or track progress.

8. Interview: "RAG or finetuning for our support bot?"

Facts that are missing or change often → RAG. Behaviour, format, style or a narrow skill → finetuning. Often both: RAG for knowledge, a small finetune (for example LoRA) for format. Try prompting first, it is cheapest (Ch.5–7).

9. Interview: "Our LLM API feels slow." Which two latency numbers do you split it into, and what affects each?

TTFT (time to first token), driven by prefilling the input; TPOT (time per output token), driven by decoding. Long prompts raise TTFT (prompt caching helps); long outputs or a big model raise TPOT (quantization, smaller model, better attention kernels help) (Ch.9).

10. Interview: "Is LLM-as-a-judge good enough for evaluation?"

Useful but subjective: scores depend on the judge model and prompt, can change when the judge is updated, and aren't comparable across judges. Pin and version the judge, and combine with exact evaluation and/or human evaluation (Ch.3).

11. What would happen if you trained your chatbot directly on thumbs-up feedback with no checks?

Risk of a degenerate feedback loop (the product drifts toward what a small early group liked) and sycophancy (the model learns to tell users what they want to hear rather than what's accurate). Ch.10 says understand feedback's limits before using it.

12. Which order of adaptation techniques does the book follow, and why is it a good interview skeleton?

Prompting → RAG and agents (input-side, no weight change) → finetuning (weight change, needs data) → inference optimisation → whole-system architecture. It goes from cheapest/fastest to most expensive, and starts from evaluation, which shows the interviewer you measure before you build.
