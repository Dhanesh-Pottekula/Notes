The preface: what this book teaches, how it teaches it, and what you need to run it

The preface is the book's instruction manual. It says the goal is intuition first: lots of pictures, few equations, and the fundamentals that stay true while the field moves fast. It sets up one distinction the whole book uses: representation models (LLMs that turn text into numbers for tasks like classification) versus generative models (LLMs that write text, like GPT). It then lays out the book in three parts (understand models → use pretrained models → train and fine-tune models), and tells you exactly what you need: some Python and ML basics, a GPU with 16 GB of VRAM (free on Google Colab's T4), a conda/pip setup, and free API keys for OpenAI, Cohere and Hugging Face. It ends with the usual O'Reilly housekeeping (typographic conventions, code-reuse permissions, contact details) and the authors' thank-yous.

01 · PREFACE (OPENING)

Why this book exists and what it covers

pdf p13

Large language models (LLMs) have had a deep, wide impact on the world. Because they let machines better understand and generate human-like language, they have opened new possibilities in AI and affected entire industries.

The book promises a comprehensive and highly visual introduction to LLMs, covering both conceptual foundations (how they work) and practical applications (what you build with them). The journey it describes, in order:

  1. History and evolution: from the word representations that existed before deep learning, up to the Transformer architecture, which the authors call cutting-edge "at the time of this writing" (2024).
  2. Inner workings: LLM architectures, training methods and fine-tuning techniques.
  3. Applications: text classification, clustering, topic modeling, chatbots, search engines "and more".

Its selling point is a blend of intuition-building, applications and an illustrative style. The authors say it is meant for both beginners and experts, and invite you to "start building with LLMs".

Step through: the book's journey in one line

The step texts paraphrase the preface's own list; the chapter pointers come from Figure P-1.

Picture it

Think of a driving school. Most schools either hand you theory (the RTO rule book) or just put you in a car. This book promises both: first you see a cut-away drawing of the engine (how a Transformer works), then you drive in the city (classification, search, chatbots), and finally you learn to tune the engine yourself (fine-tuning). Where it breaks: a driving school's car does not change every month; LLMs do, which is exactly why the authors lean on fundamentals (next plate).

What's happening behind it

The order "history → inner workings → applications" is deliberate: every application later in the book (search, RAG, classification) is built from the same two pieces explained early on, tokens and embeddings, plus the Transformer that processes them. Knowing the parts makes each application a recombination rather than a new thing to memorise.

02 · AN INTUITION-FIRST PHILOSOPHY

Learn the fundamentals, through pictures, instead of chasing every new model

pdf p13–14

The main goal of the book is to give you an intuition for the field of LLMs. The authors name the problem directly: the pace of development in Language AI is incredibly fast, and trying to keep up with every latest technology is frustrating. So instead they focus on the fundamentals of LLMs, and aim for a "fun and easy" learning process.

To get there they use visual language liberally: illustrations give a visual identity to the major concepts and processes in how LLMs learn. They call this an "illustrative method of storytelling" and hope to take you on a journey into this "potentially world-changing" field.

Footnote 1 (pdf p14) points to the source of this style: J. Alammar, "Machine learning research communication via illustrated and interactive web articles", Beyond Static Papers: Rethinking How We Share Scientific Understanding in ML, ICLR 2021 Workshop (2021). (ICLR = International Conference on Learning Representations.) Jay Alammar is known for illustrated blog posts such as "The Illustrated Transformer", which Chapter 3 updates.

Picture it

Phone models change every few months, but the idea of a touchscreen, a battery and a SIM has stayed the same for fifteen years. If you understand those, any new phone makes sense in a minute. LLM releases are the new phones; tokens, embeddings and attention are the touchscreen and battery. Where it breaks: sometimes a "fundamental" does change (the Transformer itself replaced older designs in 2017), so the book still covers history, not just the current state.

What's happening behind it

Pictures work for this topic because most LLM ideas are about shapes of data moving through steps: a sentence becomes a list of tokens, each token becomes a vector, vectors flow through layers. A diagram shows the shape and the flow at once; an equation shows only one step at a time. That is why the book leans so heavily on figures (hundreds of them).

Interview angle

"How do you keep up with the LLM field?" A crisp answer: "I anchor on the fundamentals that don't change from release to release: tokenization, embeddings, attention, the cost of context length, and evaluation. New models are then mostly a question of where they sit on quality, latency and price, which I can benchmark on my own task instead of trusting a leaderboard."

03 · REPRESENTATION VS GENERATIVE MODELS

Two kinds of language model, and why "large" is used loosely

pdf p14

Still inside "An Intuition-First Philosophy", the preface draws the distinction the book uses everywhere:

Representation modelsLLMs that do not generate text. They are commonly used for task-specific use cases, like classification. They turn text into numbers (representations) that a task can use.
Generative (generation) modelsLLMs that generate text, like the GPT models. They are usually the first thing people think of when they hear "LLM".

The authors stress that even though generative models get the attention, there is still much use for representation models.

Second point: the authors use the word "large" loosely, and often just say language models. Their reason: size labels are often arbitrary and not always indicative of capability. A bigger model is not automatically a better one for your task.

Try it: one sentence, two kinds of model (toy, modelled)

Pick a sentence and a model.

Outputs are invented to show the shape of each model's output (a vector + label vs new text). They are not from a real model.

Picture it

A kirana shop has two helpers. One is the sorter: he looks at every item and puts it on the right shelf (rice, soap, snacks). He never says a word, but the shop runs on his work. The other is the talker: he chats with customers and writes the bill. Representation models are the sorter; generative models are the talker. Where it breaks: in a real LLM, the same Transformer building blocks are used for both; the difference is in what they are trained to output, not in a different "kind of brain". Some models (Ch.1 explains encoder vs decoder) can also be adapted across the line.

What's happening behind it

A representation model reads the whole input and produces a fixed set of numbers for it (an embedding, or a score per class). That is cheap, fast and deterministic, which is why it suits classification, clustering and search. A generative model produces output one token at a time, each time feeding its own output back in; that makes it flexible (it can write anything) but slower and harder to control. Chapter 1 maps these to encoder-only models like BERT and decoder-only models like GPT.

Worked example

Routing 10,000 support tickets a day into 5 queues: a representation model gives each ticket a label in one forward pass and the output is always one of the 5 labels. Asking a generative model "which queue?" works too, but it may answer "Probably billing, though it could be…", which you then have to parse. The book returns to exactly this choice in Ch.4.

Interview angle

"When would you not use a GPT-style model?" Say: "When the task is classification, deduplication, clustering or retrieval, a representation (embedding or encoder) model is usually cheaper, faster and more stable, and its output is easy to evaluate. I'd use a generative model when the output itself must be free text, or to bootstrap labels. In a RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) memory system you use both: an embedding model to find the right memories and a generative model to write the answer."

04 · PREREQUISITES

What you need to know before starting

pdf p14

The book assumes:

  • Some experience programming in Python.
  • Familiarity with the fundamentals of machine learning.

It does not assume:

  • Prior knowledge of deep learning frameworks such as PyTorch or TensorFlow.
  • Prior knowledge of generative modeling.

The focus is on building strong intuition rather than deriving mathematical equations. Illustrations plus hands-on examples drive the learning.

If you don't know Python, the authors suggest the Learn Python website (many tutorials on the basics). To make things easier, all the code is available on Google Colab, where you can run it without installing anything locally.

Not needing PyTorch works because the book mostly uses high-level libraries (Hugging Face transformers, sentence-transformers and others in later chapters) that hide the framework. 06 · PART I: UNDERSTANDING LANGUAGE MODELS

How language models work, small and large

pdf p14–15

Part I explores the inner workings of language models, both small and large:

  1. Chapter 1: an overview of the field and common techniques.
  2. Chapter 2: two central components of these models, tokenization and embeddings.
  3. Chapter 3: an updated and expanded version of Jay's well-known "Illustrated Transformer", diving into the architecture of these models.

Many terms and definitions introduced here are used throughout the book, so this part is the vocabulary for everything after it.

Try it: the two "central components" in one toy

Type a short phrase and press the button.

Toy only (modelled): splits on spaces and makes a fake 8-number "embedding" from the letters. Real tokenizers split into sub-words (Ch.2) and real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of learned numbers.

Picture it

Tokenization is the railway reservation clerk cutting your journey into legs (Pune→Mumbai, Mumbai→Delhi); embeddings give each leg a code number that says where on the map it sits. The Transformer (Ch.3) is the whole reservation system that looks at all legs together to plan the trip. Where it breaks: a journey's legs have an obvious meaning; a token's embedding is learned from data and its numbers are not individually readable.

What's happening behind it

A model cannot read letters; it reads numbers. Tokenization decides the units, embeddings turn each unit into a vector, and the Transformer mixes those vectors so each one carries context from the others. Every later chapter is some use of those final vectors: classify them, cluster them, compare them for search, or predict the next token from them.

Step through: one product, built with Part II chapters only

Scenario is mine (modelled), to show how the chapters combine; it is not from the book.

Picture it

Renting a fully equipped kitchen versus building one. Part II is renting: the stove and oven (pretrained models) already work, you just learn recipes (classification, search, prompting). Where it breaks: a rented kitchen never learns your recipes; a pretrained model also won't learn your data unless you give it at request time (RAG, Ch.8) or fine-tune it (Part III).

What's happening behind it

Pretrained models already contain general language skill learned from huge text collections. Most use cases need only a way to point that skill at a task: an embedding plus a simple classifier, a clustering algorithm over embeddings, a well-written prompt, or retrieved documents placed into the prompt. None of these change the model's weights, which keeps them cheap and easy to swap.

Interview angle

"Design a support bot over our docs" is a classic system-design prompt. Part II is the whole answer outline: chunk and embed the docs, store vectors, retrieve top-k for each question, prompt a generative model with the retrieved text, and classify or route requests the bot should not answer. Mention evaluation (retrieval hit rate, answer faithfulness) to stand out.

08 · PART III: TRAINING AND FINE-TUNING LANGUAGE MODELS

Changing the model itself

pdf p16

Part III explores advanced concepts through training and fine-tuning all kinds of language models:

  1. Chapter 10: how to create and fine-tune an embedding model.
  2. Chapter 11: how to fine-tune BERT for classification.
  3. Chapter 12: several methods for fine-tuning generation models.

Notice the pattern: Part III fine-tunes one of each kind from plate 03. Ch.10 and Ch.11 are representation models (embeddings, BERT); Ch.12 is generative models.

Picture it

A tailor altering a ready-made kurta. The kurta is already 90% right (pretraining); alteration (fine-tuning) shortens the sleeves to fit you. You'd never weave new cloth (train from scratch) just to fix the sleeves. Where it breaks: a bad alteration on a kurta is visible; a bad fine-tune can quietly make the model worse at things you didn't test ("forgetting").

What's happening behind it

Fine-tuning continues training a pretrained model on a smaller, task-specific dataset, so its weights shift toward your task. Because the model already knows language, a few thousand good examples can be enough, where training from scratch would need billions of tokens.

09 · HARDWARE AND SOFTWARE REQUIREMENTS

A GPU with 16 GB of VRAM, free on Google Colab

pdf p16

Running generative models is generally compute-intensive and needs a computer with a strong GPU (graphics processing unit). Not every reader has one, so all examples are made to run on an online platform: Google Colaboratory, usually shortened to "Google Colab".

At the time of writing, Colab gives you an NVIDIA T4 GPU for free. The T4 has 16 GB of VRAM (VRAM = video RAM, "the memory of your GPU"). 16 GB of VRAM is the minimum the authors expect for the examples in the book.

Note (book's callout, pdf p16)

Not every chapter needs 16 GB. Some examples, like training and fine-tuning, are more compute-intensive than others, such as prompt engineering. The repository lists the minimum GPU requirements for each chapter.

All code, requirements and extra tutorials are in the book's repository. To run things locally, the authors recommend an NVIDIA GPU with at least 16 GB of VRAM. For a local install, for example with conda, create the environment like this:

conda create -n thellmbook python=3.10
conda activate thellmbook
  • conda create -n thellmbook python=3.10: makes a new isolated environment named thellmbook with Python 3.10, so the book's pinned library versions don't clash with your other projects.
  • conda activate thellmbook: switches your shell into that environment; every pip install after this goes into it.

Then fork or clone the repository and, inside the new Python 3.10 environment, install all dependencies:

pip install -r requirements.txt

-r requirements.txt tells pip to read the list of packages (and versions) from the repo's file and install them all at once.

Try it: will a model fit in 16 GB? (Beyond the book, modelled)

The preface only gives the 16 GB number. This calculator uses the common rule of thumb weights memory ≈ parameters × bytes per parameter, plus a margin for activations and the KV cache (key-value cache). Quantization is covered in later chapters.

weights: –with overhead: –T4 (16 GB): –

Modelled estimate for inference only. Fine-tuning needs much more (gradients and optimizer state), which is why the note above singles out training chapters.

Picture it

VRAM is the size of the kitchen counter, not the size of the pantry. The whole model (the ingredients) has to fit on the counter at once to cook quickly; your laptop's normal RAM is the pantry down the hall. Colab lends you a 16 GB counter for free. Where it breaks: unlike a counter, you can "shrink" the ingredients (quantization, lower precision) so a larger model fits.

What's happening behind it

A GPU is fast because thousands of small cores do matrix maths in parallel, but they can only work quickly on data that is in the GPU's own memory. Every model weight is read for every generated token, so the weights must sit in VRAM. That is why VRAM size, more than GPU speed, decides which models you can run at all.

Worked example (modelled)

A 7-billion-parameter model in 16-bit needs about 7 × 2 = 14 GB just for weights; add 20% and it is ~16.8 GB, so it doesn't fit a T4. The same model in 4-bit needs ~3.5 GB of weights and fits easily. A ~3.8B model in 16-bit (~7.6 GB) fits with room to spare.

Interview angle

"How much GPU memory do you need to serve model X?" Say: "Parameters times bytes per parameter for the weights, so 7B in fp16 is about 14 GB, then add KV cache, which grows with batch size times context length, plus activations. If it doesn't fit, quantize, use a smaller model, or shard across GPUs." For a realtime voice agent, add that latency, not just fit, decides the GPU.

10 · API KEYS

Free accounts for OpenAI, Cohere and Hugging Face

pdf p17

The book uses both open source and proprietary models, on purpose, to show the advantages and disadvantages of both. For the proprietary models, from OpenAI and Cohere, you need to create a free account:

OpenAIClick "sign up" on the site to create a free account. The account lets you create an API key, which can be used to access GPT-3.5. Then go to "API keys" to create a secret key.
CohereRegister a free account on the website. Then go to "API keys" to create a secret key.
Hugging FaceClick "sign up" on the Hugging Face website for a free account. Then in "Settings" go to "Access Tokens" to create a token you can use to download certain LLMs.

With both OpenAI and Cohere, rate limits apply: the free keys only allow a limited number of calls per minute. The authors took that into account in all examples and provided local alternatives where necessary.

For the open source models you do not need an account, with one exception: the Llama 2 model in Chapter 2 needs a Hugging Face account (beyond the book: Llama 2 is a "gated" model whose licence you accept on Hugging Face before downloading).

Beyond the book: keeping keys safe in code 
import os
api_key = os.environ["OPENAI_API_KEY"]   # read from the environment, never hard-code

Never paste a secret key into a notebook you share or a repo you push. In Colab, the "Secrets" panel or an environment variable keeps it out of the code. Also beyond the book: GPT-3.5 has since been superseded by newer OpenAI models, and model names in the code may need updating.

Picture it

An API key is like a UPI PIN tied to your account: anyone who has it can spend your quota. A rate limit is the bank's "max transactions per minute" rule: hit it and further payments are refused until the window resets. Where it breaks: a UPI PIN is typed by you each time; an API key sits in your code or environment and is sent with every request automatically, so leaking it is easier.

What's happening behind it

Proprietary models run on the provider's servers; you send text over HTTPS and the key tells them who to bill and which limits to apply. Open source models are files you download and run on your own GPU (here Colab's T4), so no key is needed, except where the model owner requires an account first, as the book says for Llama 2 (beyond the book: Meta gates it behind a licence acceptance on Hugging Face).

Interview angle

"Open source vs proprietary model for production?" Answer with the trade-off the book sets up: "Proprietary APIs give top quality with zero infrastructure but you pay per token, you're bound by rate limits and data leaves your network. Open models need GPUs and ops work, but you control cost at scale, latency, privacy and fine-tuning. Many teams put an LLM gateway in front of both so they can route, retry on 429s (rate-limit errors) and fall back between providers."

Summary

Σ preface in plain words
  • The book is a visual, intuition-first introduction to LLMs: history, inner workings, training/fine-tuning, and applications.
  • It focuses on fundamentals because the field moves too fast to chase every new model.
  • It separates representation models (no text generation; classification and similar tasks) from generative models (write text, like GPT). Both matter. "Large" is used loosely; size doesn't guarantee capability.
  • You need Python and ML basics; you don't need PyTorch, TensorFlow or generative-modeling experience.
  • Three parts: understand (Ch.1–3), use pretrained (Ch.4–9), train and fine-tune (Ch.10–12). Chapters stand alone.
  • Examples run on Google Colab's free T4 (16 GB VRAM); locally use a 16 GB NVIDIA GPU, a conda Python 3.10 env and pip install -r requirements.txt. Per-chapter GPU minimums are in the repo.
  • Free OpenAI and Cohere keys (rate-limited) for proprietary models; a Hugging Face token for Llama 2 in Ch.2.
  • Code is reusable in your own programs; selling/distributing or bulk-copying into docs needs permission. Attribution appreciated.

Check yourself

click to reveal
1. What is the book's main goal, and why did the authors choose it?

To give an intuition for LLMs. The field moves so fast that chasing the latest technology is frustrating, so they focus on fundamentals, taught visually.

2. Define a representation model and a generative model.

Representation: an LLM that doesn't generate text, used for task-specific work like classification. Generative: an LLM that generates text, like GPT.

3. Why do the authors often drop the word "large"?

Size descriptions are often arbitrary and not always indicative of capability.

4. Which two things does the book NOT expect you to know?

Deep learning frameworks like PyTorch or TensorFlow, and generative modeling.

5. Name the three parts and which chapters belong to each.

Part I Understanding Language Models (1–3), Part II Using Pretrained Language Models (4–9), Part III Training and Fine-Tuning Language Models (10–12).

6. What does Part II never do to the models?

Fine-tune them. It uses pretrained models as they are.

7. Which chapter covers semantic search and RAG, and why might the preface confuse you?

Chapter 8 (Figure P-1). The preface sentence says Chapter 6 for semantic search, which doesn't match the actual chapter list (Ch.6 is Prompt Engineering).

8. What GPU and how much VRAM does the book expect?

An NVIDIA GPU with at least 16 GB VRAM, e.g. the free T4 on Google Colab.

9. What if a chapter needs less than 16 GB?

The repo lists minimum GPU requirements per chapter; prompt engineering needs less than training/fine-tuning.

10. Write the commands for a local setup.

conda create -n thellmbook python=3.10, conda activate thellmbook, then in the cloned repo pip install -r requirements.txt.

11. Which accounts are needed, and for what?

OpenAI (key for GPT-3.5) and Cohere (key) for proprietary models; Hugging Face (access token) only for Llama 2 in Ch.2. Other open models need no account.

12. What would happen if you looped 500 OpenAI calls on a free key?

You'd hit the rate limit (limited calls per minute) and calls would be refused. The book designed its examples around this and gives local alternatives.

13. Can you use several chunks of the book's code in your company's internal tool without asking?

Yes. Using several chunks in a program needs no permission. Selling/distributing examples, or putting a significant amount into product documentation, does.

14. Interview: "Your team wants to fine-tune a model for a support bot. What do you ask first?"

Whether a pretrained model with good prompts and retrieval (the book's Part II approach) already solves it. Fine-tune (Part III) only if you need consistent style/format/behaviour or a cheaper smaller model, and you have good training data and an evaluation set.

15. Interview: "Would a 7B model in 16-bit fit on a T4 for inference?" (beyond the book)

Barely not: about 14 GB of weights plus KV cache and activations exceeds 16 GB in practice. Quantize to 8- or 4-bit, or pick a smaller model.
