Text clustering and topic modeling: finding groups in text nobody has labelled

Classification (Chapter 4) needs labels. This chapter asks what you can do without any labels: take 44,949 research-paper abstracts and let the machine find the groups by itself. The recipe has three steps: turn every document into an embedding, squeeze those embeddings down to a few dimensions with UMAP, and let HDBSCAN find dense groups (and leave the odd ones out as outliers). Clusters alone have no names, so the second half builds BERTopic, which describes each cluster with keywords using an old-school trick, a cluster-level bag-of-words weighted by c-TF-IDF. Because every step is a swappable "Lego block", you can then put smarter blocks on top: KeyBERTInspired and MMR rerank the keywords, and a generative LLM (Flan-T5, GPT-3.5) writes a readable label, called once per topic instead of once per document.

01 Why cluster text? (chapter opening)

pdf p159–160 · Fig 5-1, 5-2

For the last few years supervised techniques (you have labelled examples, like "this review is positive") such as classification have ruled industry. The authors argue that unsupervised techniques (no labels at all) such as text clustering are just as valuable and should not be underestimated.

Text clustering means grouping texts that are similar in their semantic content: their meaning and the relationships inside them. The result (Figure 5-1) is a set of clusters, each holding semantically similar documents. That gives you two things at once:

  • efficient categorisation of large volumes of unstructured text, and
  • quick exploratory data analysis: you see what is in a pile of text before you read it.

How to read this figure. Left: a pile of plain documents (the input, with no labels). Right: each document has become a point on a grid. A document about cats lands right next to one about dogs (purple blob), soccer next to basketball (blue), pasta next to pizza (green). Nobody told the algorithm "pets", "sports" or "food"; closeness in meaning alone made the groups.

Why does it work better now? Newer language models give contextual and semantic representations of text. "Language is more than a bag of words", and these models capture that. Because clustering does not need supervision, it allows creative uses the book lists:

  • finding outliers (documents that fit no group),
  • speeding up labelling (label a whole cluster at once instead of one document at a time),
  • finding incorrectly labelled data (a "positive" review sitting inside a cluster of negative ones is suspicious).

Clustering also leads into topic modeling: discovering the (abstract) topics that appear in a large collection of text. As Figure 5-2 shows, a topic is usually described with keywords or keyphrases and, ideally, one overarching label.

How to read this figure. Same three blobs as Figure 5-1, but each now has a label in bold (Pets, Food, Sports) and a list of keywords in italics ("pet, dog, cat, animal shelter, …"). Clustering gave us the groups; topic modeling gives them meaning. Notice that keywords can include words not in the two example documents ("rice", "animal shelter"): they summarise the whole cluster.

Plan of the chapter: first do clustering with embedding models, then move to a clustering-inspired topic modeling method called BERTopic. The authors say this chapter is important for the whole book because it shows how to combine different kinds of language models: encoder-only models (for embeddings), decoder-only models (generative), and even classical methods (bag-of-words) into new pipelines.

Picture it

A raddiwala (scrap dealer) gets a truck of mixed paper with no labels. He doesn't know beforehand which piles he'll need; he just puts newspapers with newspapers, cardboard with cardboard, notebooks with notebooks, and keeps a small "odd stuff" heap. That's clustering. Writing "NEWSPAPER — ₹14/kg" on each pile is topic modeling.

Where it breaks: the raddiwala sorts by what paper is (one property). Text clustering sorts by meaning across hundreds of hidden properties at once, and the piles can be fuzzy at the edges.

What's happening behind it

Clustering relies on one assumption: distance between representations ≈ difference in meaning. If the embedding model puts "cat" and "dog" documents near each other and far from "pizza" documents, any algorithm that looks for crowded regions will find the groups. So the quality of the clusters is capped by the quality of the representation. That is exactly why better language models made clustering better.

Interview angle

"When would you use clustering instead of classification?" — "When I don't have labels yet, or don't even know the categories. I'd embed the texts, cluster them, and read samples per cluster. That tells me what categories exist, how hard the task is, and lets me label a whole cluster at once. It also surfaces outliers and mislabelled data." In a voice-agent product this is how you'd mine thousands of call transcripts for the intents users actually have before building an intent classifier.

03 A common pipeline for text clustering

pdf p161

Clustering lets you discover patterns in data that you may or may not know about. It gives an intuitive feel for a task (for example, a classification task you're about to build) and for how complex that task is. So clustering is more than a quick exploratory tool.

There are many clustering methods, from graph-based neural networks to centroid-based techniques. But one pipeline has become popular, with three steps and three algorithms:

  1. Embed: convert the input documents to embeddings with an embedding model.
  2. Reduce: shrink the number of dimensions of the embeddings with a dimensionality reduction model.
  3. Cluster: find groups of semantically similar documents with a cluster model.
step 0 / 3
Press Next to push the documents through the pipeline.
Picture it

Sorting wedding guests into tables: (1) for every guest write a long profile card (embed), (2) boil each card down to a few key traits like "family side, age group, language" (reduce), (3) seat people who share traits together and leave the one stranger at the side (cluster).

Where it breaks: a caterer decides the number of tables first. The density-based clusterer we'll use does not; it discovers how many tables there should be.

What's happening behind it

Each step fixes a weakness of the next. Clustering algorithms need numbers, so we embed. Clustering on 384 numbers works badly (distances become less meaningful in high dimensions), so we reduce. Only then do we cluster. Because the three steps only talk to each other through arrays (documents → matrix → smaller matrix → labels), any one of them can be replaced. That property becomes BERTopic's big selling point later.

Interview angle

"Walk me through how you'd cluster 1M support tickets." — "Embed with a sentence-embedding model chosen for clustering on MTEB, reduce to ~5–10 dimensions with UMAP using cosine distance, then HDBSCAN so I don't have to pick the number of clusters and noise stays as outliers. Then I name clusters with c-TF-IDF keywords and spot-check samples by hand."

04 Step 1: embedding documents

pdf p161–162 · Fig 5-3

The first step turns text into embeddings. Recall from earlier chapters: an embedding is a list of numbers that tries to capture the meaning of a text.

How to read this figure. On the left, n documents. On the right, an orange grid: one row per document (the vertical arrow, "number of documents") and one column per dimension (the horizontal arrow, "e.g., 512 values"). Every document, short or long, becomes a row of the same width. The "512" is just an example; the model actually used gives 384.

Choosing the model. For clustering, pick an embedding model optimised for semantic similarity, because we are looking for groups of semantically similar documents. Luckily, at the time of writing, most embedding models focus on exactly that.

As in Chapter 4, the authors use the MTEB (Massive Text Embedding Benchmark) leaderboard to choose. They want a model that scores decently on clustering tasks but is small enough to run quickly. Instead of Chapter 4's sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2, they use thenlper/gte-small: it is more recent, it beats the older model on clustering tasks, and being small it is even faster at inference. They invite you to try newer models released since.

# Check the dimensions of the resulting embeddings

  • .encode(abstracts, ...) runs all 44,949 abstracts through the model (in batches) and returns a NumPy array.

Picture it

An embedding is like a 384-question survey every document fills in: "how much are you about speech? about medicine? about sentiment?…" (the real questions are not human-readable). Two documents that answer similarly are about similar things.

Where it breaks: no single dimension means "speech". Meaning is spread across all 384 numbers together.

What's happening behind it

Sentence-embedding models like gte-small are encoder models trained (Chapter 10 shows how) so that texts with similar meaning end up with a high cosine similarity. That training objective is why "optimised for semantic similarity" matters: a model trained for, say, next-token prediction does not promise that distance equals meaning, so clusters built on it would be noisier.

Interview angle

"How do you choose an embedding model?" — "Look at MTEB for the task family I care about (clustering, retrieval, classification), then weigh size and latency. For clustering 45k abstracts a 384-dim small model like gte-small is plenty and fast. Then validate on my own data, because leaderboard scores don't always transfer." Same reasoning applies to choosing the embedder for a RAG memory store.

05 Step 2: reducing the dimensionality of embeddings

pdf p162–164 · Fig 5-4, 5-5

Before clustering we must deal with the high dimensionality of the embeddings. As the number of dimensions grows, the number of possible values grows exponentially, and finding all the subspaces within each dimension gets more and more complex. So high-dimensional data is troublesome for many clustering techniques: it gets harder to find meaningful clusters.

The fix is dimensionality reduction: represent the same data with fewer dimensions (Figure 5-4). These techniques try to preserve the global structure of the high-dimensional data while finding a low-dimensional representation.

How to read this figure. Left: points live in 3 dimensions (x, y, z). Right: the same points after reduction, now with only 2 coordinates, called a and b (new axes, not x and y). The purple group stays mostly on the left and green mostly on the right: the structure survived. But look closely: one purple point now sits among green ones near the bottom. That is the information loss the book warns about.

Important: this is compression. The algorithm does not arbitrarily delete dimensions (it doesn't just throw away z). It builds new axes that keep as much of the structure as possible. So the second step of our pipeline is dimensionality reduction (Figure 5-5).

How to read this figure. Same number of rows (documents) on both sides; only the width changes, from "e.g. 512 values" to "e.g. 3 values" of compressed dimensions. The paler colour hints that each compressed row carries a squeezed, lossy version of the original meaning. (In the actual code it's 384 → 5.)

Well-known methods: PCA (Principal Component Analysis, Hotelling 1933) and UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection, McInnes, Healy & Melville 2018). The authors pick UMAP because it tends to handle nonlinear relationships and structures a bit better than PCA.

Book warning

Dimensionality reduction is not flawless. It cannot perfectly capture high-dimensional data in fewer dimensions; information will always be lost. There's a balance between reducing dimensions and keeping as much information as possible.

from umap import UMAP

# We reduce the input embeddings from 384 dimensions to 5 dimensions
umap_model = UMAP(
    n_components=5, min_dist=0.0, metric='cosine', random_state=42
)
reduced_embeddings = umap_model.fit_transform(embeddings)
ParameterValueWhat it does and why (book)
n_components5Size of the lower-dimensional space. Generally 5 to 10 works well to capture high-dimensional global structure.
min_dist0.0Minimum distance between embedded points. 0 generally gives tighter clusters (points may pack together), which helps the clusterer.
metric'cosine'Euclidean-based methods have issues with high-dimensional data, so measure closeness by angle (cosine) in the 384-dim input.
random_state42Makes results reproducible across sessions, but disables parallelism, so training is slower.

fit_transform learns the low-dimensional layout and returns it: reduced_embeddings is a 44,949 × 5 array.

Try it: squash 3-D points onto 2-D (modelled)

Two groups of points live in 3-D. Turn the "camera" to choose which 2-D view to keep. Some views keep the groups apart; some pile them on top of each other. A good reduction method searches for a view (or a bent, nonlinear map, in UMAP's case) that keeps the groups apart. This toy uses a straight projection, closer in spirit to PCA.

Picture it

A shadow puppet. Your 3-D hand becomes a 2-D shadow on the wall. Hold it at the right angle and the shadow is clearly a deer; at the wrong angle it's a blob. Dimensionality reduction is choosing (or bending) the light so the shadow keeps the shape. From 384 dimensions down to 5 the "shadow" is still a lossy copy.

Where it breaks: a shadow is a straight (linear) projection, like PCA. UMAP can bend space, more like unfolding a crumpled paper, which is why it copes better with nonlinear structure.

What's happening behind it

PCA finds the straight directions along which the data varies most and keeps the top few. UMAP (beyond the book's detail) first builds a graph of each point's nearest neighbours in the original space, then lays out points in the small space so that the same neighbours stay neighbours. That keeps local groups intact even if the data lies on a curved surface. min_dist=0 lets UMAP pack neighbours very close, which is ideal for a density-based clusterer that looks for crowded regions.

Worked example

Input 44,949 × 384 = 17.3 million numbers. Output 44,949 × 5 = 224,745 numbers, about 77× fewer. HDBSCAN now measures distances in 5-D, where "near" and "far" are much more distinct than in 384-D. (Arithmetic is modelled from the book's shapes.)

Interview angle

"Why not cluster the raw 384-dim embeddings?" — "In high dimensions distances concentrate, so density-based clustering struggles to tell dense from sparse. UMAP to 5–10 dims with cosine metric and min_dist=0 keeps neighbourhood structure and makes clusters tight. The cost is some information loss, so I'd still sanity-check clusters by reading samples. And setting random_state makes it reproducible but single-threaded."

06 Step 3: clustering the reduced embeddings

pdf p164–166 · Fig 5-6, 5-7

The third step clusters the reduced embeddings (Figure 5-6).

How to read this figure. Each row of the small table becomes a point. The clusterer draws three blobs around crowded areas. Notice the two points that stay outside any blob (top middle and bottom right): they are already a preview of outliers.

Centroid vs density. A common choice is a centroid-based algorithm such as k-means, which needs you to say how many clusters to make. We don't know the number of clusters beforehand. A density-based algorithm instead works out the number of clusters itself and does not force every point into a cluster (Figure 5-7).

How to read this figure. Same points, two algorithms. Left (centroid-based): the space is cut into regions like a map of states; every point, even the lonely one at the top or bottom-right, is forced into the nearest region. Right (density-based): only crowded areas become clusters; the lonely points are labelled Outlier (not assigned to any cluster). The caption's point: the algorithm changes not just the groups but how you see the data.

A common density-based model is HDBSCAN (Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise; McInnes, Healy & Astels 2017). It is a hierarchical version of DBSCAN (Ester et al., KDD 1996) and finds dense (micro-)clusters without you specifying the number of clusters. Being density-based, it also detects outliers: points that belong to no cluster. They are not assigned or forced anywhere; in other words, they are ignored. Since ArXiv contains some niche papers, a model that detects outliers is helpful.

from hdbscan import HDBSCAN

# We fit the model and extract the clusters
hdbscan_model = HDBSCAN(
    min_cluster_size=50, metric="euclidean", cluster_selection_method="eom"
).fit(reduced_embeddings)
clusters = hdbscan_model.labels_

# How many clusters did we generate?
len(set(clusters))
156
  • min_cluster_size=50: the smallest a cluster may be. Lower it to get more (smaller) clusters.
  • metric="euclidean": plain straight-line distance is fine now, because we are in the 5-D space (the high-dimension problem was handled by UMAP).
  • cluster_selection_method="eom": "excess of mass", HDBSCAN's default rule for choosing which clusters from its hierarchy to keep (the book doesn't explain it; this gloss is beyond the book).
  • labels_: one integer per document; -1 means outlier.
  • len(set(clusters)) = 156 distinct labels. As the topic table later shows (13), that is 155 real clusters (0 to 154) plus the -1 outlier label.

Try it: centroid-based vs density-based (modelled)

Toy 2-D data: three groups plus a few stray points. k-means must use exactly k clusters and places every point. The density rule (a simplified DBSCAN-style rule, not full HDBSCAN) calls a point "core" if at least min size points lie within the radius, grows clusters from core points, and leaves the rest as grey outliers.

Picture it

k-means is a school principal who says "we have exactly 3 houses, every student joins one", so even the new kid who knows nobody gets assigned. HDBSCAN is a college fest: groups form around people who already hang out together, and the loner by the chai stall is simply left alone.

Where it breaks: HDBSCAN's "loners" can be a big share of the data (here about a third of the papers), which is more than you'd expect at a fest.

What's happening behind it

DBSCAN uses one fixed radius: a region is a cluster if it has enough neighbours within that radius. One radius can't fit both very dense and loose groups. HDBSCAN avoids picking a radius by trying all of them, building a hierarchy (a tree) of how clusters split as density increases, and then keeping the clusters that are stable over a wide range of densities, subject to min_cluster_size. That is why you only give it a minimum size and it finds the count itself.

Worked example

With 44,949 documents and min_cluster_size=50, the maximum possible number of clusters is 44,949 ÷ 50 ≈ 898. HDBSCAN found 155 (plus outliers), so most clusters are well above the minimum: the topic table shows the largest real cluster has 2,290 papers and the smallest has 50.

Interview angle

"k-means or HDBSCAN?" — "k-means if I know or must fix the number of groups and want every item assigned, for example routing every ticket to one of 8 queues. HDBSCAN when I'm exploring: it finds the number of clusters, handles uneven densities, and marks noise as -1 instead of polluting clusters. The trade-off is many outliers, which I can reassign later if needed."

Picture it

A night-time satellite photo of India: bright patches are cities (clusters), the faint glow between them is scattered villages (outliers). You can see where people are, but the photo doesn't tell you which city is Pune and which is Surat. For that you need labels, which is the next section.

Where it breaks: the photo's distances are real. UMAP's 2-D distances are distorted, so two "cities" next to each other may not be similar at all.

What's happening behind it

The 2-D UMAP is fit separately from the 5-D one. Both preserve neighbourhoods, so the same clusters usually show up as patches, but squeezing to 2-D forces some overlap and stretching. That's why the clusters come from the 5-D space and the picture from the 2-D one: never cluster on the plotting projection, and never trust the plot alone.

Worked example

From the topic table in 13: 14,520 of 44,949 papers are outliers, which is 32% (computed from the book's numbers). So about a third of the dots in Figure 5-8 are the grey haze. That's normal for HDBSCAN on text, and it's one reason the book later mentions reduce_outliers().

"How do you evaluate clusters without labels?" — "Mostly by inspection: read samples from each cluster, check the outlier share, look at a 2-D map but don't trust its distances. If I have a few labels I can check purity, and there are internal metrics, but the book's point stands: human evaluation is a key part of cluster analysis."

08 From text clustering to topic modeling

pdf p168–170 · Fig 5-9, 5-10

Clustering finds structure in large collections. We could inspect each cluster by hand and name it, as we did with the sign-language cluster: its topic is "sign language".

Finding themes or latent topics (hidden, not written anywhere explicitly) in a collection of text is called topic modeling. Traditionally it means finding a set of keywords or phrases that best represent the meaning of each topic (Figure 5-9).

How to read this figure. The output of topic modeling is not a picture of points but a list of topic representations, one row of keyword chips per topic. The dotted bracket at the bottom says representations can be keywords, bi-grams (two-word phrases), labels, etc.: BERTopic will later produce all of these.

Instead of the label "sign language", such techniques give keywords like "sign", "language", "translation". That doesn't give a single label; the user has to work out the meaning from the keywords.

Classic approaches such as latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) (Blei, Ng & Jordan, 2003) assume each topic is a probability distribution over the words of the corpus's vocabulary. Figure 5-10 shows every vocabulary word scored for its relevance to each topic.

How to read this figure. Rows are the whole vocabulary (athletes, cat, cooking, …). Each column is one topic; bar length is how strongly that word belongs to that topic. Every word has some weight in every topic (tiny bars), but each topic's keywords are the few with long, dark bars. Reading down a column gives you the topic's keyword list.

These approaches generally use bag-of-words as the main features, which ignores context and meaning of words and phrases. Our clustering example takes both into account, because it uses Transformer-based embeddings trained for semantic similarity and contextual meaning through attention.

A topic as keywords is like the Swiggy search tags on a restaurant: "biryani, kebab, haleem". You can guess it's a Hyderabadi place, but nobody wrote "Hyderabadi" for you. A label would be the sign board.

Where it breaks: a restaurant chooses its tags; topic keywords are computed from word statistics and can include junk words like "the" (we'll see this happen).

What's happening behind it

LDA imagines each document as a mix of topics and each topic as a bag of word probabilities, then fits those probabilities to the word counts. Because it only sees counts, "bank" in "river bank" and "bank loan" is the same feature. Embedding-based clustering sees the whole sentence through attention, so the two uses land in different places. BERTopic's trick (next plates) is to group with embeddings but describe with counts.

Interview angle

"LDA vs BERTopic?" — "LDA is a generative model over bag-of-words: fast, well understood, but it ignores context and needs the number of topics. BERTopic clusters contextual embeddings, so groups follow meaning, finds the topic count via HDBSCAN, and then uses class-based TF-IDF to pick keywords. It's also modular: I can add an LLM to write labels."

10 BERTopic part 2: from bag-of-words to c-TF-IDF

pdf p170–172 · Fig 5-12, 5-13, 5-14, 5-15

Step two models a distribution over the words of the corpus's vocabulary using a classic method, bag-of-words. As briefly covered in Chapter 1, bag-of-words does what its name says: it counts how many times each word appears in a document (Figure 5-12). The result can be used to find the most frequent words of a document.

How to read this figure. The yellow chips at the bottom are the vocabulary of all input documents: that, is, a, cute, dog, my, cat. Each sentence becomes one row with a count per vocabulary word. "My cat is cute" → 0 1 0 1 0 1 1 (no "that", one "is", no "a", one "cute", no "dog", one "my", one "cat"). Purple cells are non-zero. Word order is gone; only counts remain. This is also called term frequency.

Try it: your own bag-of-words

Lower-cased, split on spaces and punctuation; vocabulary in order of first appearance across B then A, like the figure. (scikit-learn's CountVectorizer sorts the vocabulary alphabetically and, by default, drops one-letter words like "a"; the toy keeps them.)

Two caveats.

  1. Document level vs cluster level. Bag-of-words describes one document; we want to describe a cluster. Fix: count word frequencies across the entire cluster instead of per document (Figure 5-13). This is c-TF, class-based term frequency.
  2. Stop words. Words like "the" and "I" appear everywhere and carry little meaning. BERTopic uses a class-based variant of TF-IDF (term frequency–inverse document frequency), c-TF-IDF, to put more weight on words meaningful to one cluster and less weight on words used across all clusters.

How to read this figure. The two sentences from Figure 5-12 are now one cluster (purple). Their counts are added: "is" appears once in each, so 2; "cute" too, so 2; the rest 1. Two more clusters (blue, green; their documents shown as "…") have their own rows. The blue cluster says "cute" 18 times. Bottom left: the notation ‖tfx,c‖ = frequency of word x in cluster c. Each row is now a "mega-document" for one cluster.

The IDF part. Each word's c-TF is multiplied by that word's IDF value. As Figure 5-14 shows, IDF is the logarithm of the average frequency of all words across all clusters divided by the total frequency of each word.

How to read this figure. The coloured boxes are each word's total count across all clusters (cfx): the column sums of Figure 5-13 (that 1+4+4 = 9, cute 2+18+2 = 22, cat 1+0+0 = 1, …). The big number on top, A = 29, is the "average frequency". The formula is IDFx = log(A / cfx + 1). Rare words (dog, cat: total 1) get a big weight; common words (cute: 22) get a small one. The +1 appears only in the figure, not in the prose; it keeps the weight positive. Note: 29 is the number the figure draws; it isn't obviously the mean of the figure's own counts (the three cluster totals are 9, 36 and 13, mean ≈ 19.3), so treat it as illustrative.

The result is a weight ("IDF") per word, multiplied by its frequency ("c-TF") to get the weighted values ("c-TF-IDF"). This second part (Figure 5-15) produces a distribution over words, like the topic distributions we saw for LDA. The bag-of-words (term frequency) can be made with scikit-learn's CountVectorizer. Here, each cluster is treated as a topic that has its own ranking of the corpus's vocabulary.

How to read this figure. Numbering continues from part 1 (steps 1–3): 4. CountVectorizer builds the class-based bag-of-words matrix (rows = clusters), 5. c-TF-IDF weighs it. The formula on the right in full: Wx,c = ‖tfx,c‖ × log(A / cfx + 1), with the c-TF part and the IDF part bracketed separately.

W(x, c) = c-TF × IDF = ‖tf(x,c)‖ × log( A / cf(x) + 1 ) tf(x,c) = count of word x in all documents of cluster c cf(x) = count of word x across all clusters A = average frequency (the book's figure uses 29)

Putting it together: the higher a word's weight in a topic, the more representative it is of that topic.

Worked example (from the figure's numbers)

IDF(cat) = ln(29/1 + 1) = ln 30 ≈ 3.40. IDF(cute) = ln(29/22 + 1) = ln 2.32 ≈ 0.84. In the purple cluster, cat has c-TF 1 and cute has c-TF 2, so W(cat) = 1 × 3.40 = 3.40 and W(cute) = 2 × 0.84 = 1.68. Even though "cute" appears twice as often in that cluster, "cat" wins (tied with "dog", which also appears once in total and so gets the same 3.40), because "cute" is everywhere (22 times overall, mostly in the blue cluster). In the blue cluster, cute has c-TF 18, so W = 18 × 0.84 ≈ 15.2: it is still that cluster's top word, because it's so dominant there.

Beyond the book

In the BERTopic library, the ‖·‖ bars in the formula mean the c-TF row is L1-normalised (each cluster's counts are divided by that cluster's total, so big clusters don't get bigger weights just for having more text), and A is the average number of words per class. The library's IDF is computed as log(1 + A / fx). This is why real topic weights in 13 are small numbers like 0.028.

Picture it

Each cluster is a WhatsApp group; c-TF is how often each word is said in that group. "Good morning" is posted in every family group, so it tells you nothing (low IDF). "Fantasy XI captain" is said a lot in only one group: that's the cricket group. c-TF-IDF finds the words that are common here and rare elsewhere.

Where it breaks: c-TF-IDF only counts words. It can't tell that "captain" and "skipper" mean the same, and abbreviations stay unexpanded.

What's happening behind it

Classic TF-IDF asks "how special is this word to this document?" and divides by how many documents contain it. c-TF-IDF does the same at cluster level: glue each cluster's documents into one big document, count, then down-weight words that are frequent across clusters. Because it only needs counts per cluster, it's very fast and doesn't care how the clusters were made (embedding, k-means, even hand labels).

Interview angle

"How does BERTopic name its topics?" — "After clustering, it concatenates all documents in a cluster, builds a bag-of-words with CountVectorizer, and weights each word with class-based TF-IDF: term frequency in the cluster times log(1 + average frequency / the word's frequency across all clusters). Top-weighted words become the topic's keywords. It's cheap and independent of the clustering model."

11 The full pipeline and its Lego blocks

pdf p172–173 · Fig 5-16, 5-17

Put the two steps together, clustering and topic representation, and you have the full BERTopic pipeline (Figure 5-16): cluster semantically similar documents, then generate topics represented by several keywords.

How to read this figure. Five boxes in a row, grouped in two dashed frames. The left frame makes the groups; the right frame names them. The only thing passed between the frames is the cluster label of each document.

A major advantage: the two steps are largely independent. c-TF-IDF does not depend on which models made the clusters. That gives modularity throughout every component, and it's a great starting point for fine-tuning the topic representations later in the chapter.

As Figure 5-17 shows, sentence-transformers is the default embedding model, but you can swap it for any embedding technique. The same goes for every other step. Don't want outliers from HDBSCAN? Use k-means instead.

15 Adding a special Lego block (representation models)

pdf p178–180 · Fig 5-19, 5-20, 5-21

The pipeline so far is fast and modular, but it has a disadvantage: a topic is still represented by a bag-of-words, with no regard for semantic structure.

The solution keeps bag-of-words' strength, its speed at producing a meaningful first representation, and then tweaks that first representation with more powerful but slower techniques such as embedding models. Figure 5-19: rerank the initial word distribution to improve it. This idea of reranking an initial set of results is a staple of neural search, covered in Chapter 8.

How to read this figure. Left: a summarization topic's top words from c-TF-IDF, longest bar first. Blue bars are words the reranker will keep near the top (Summarization, Summaries, Abstractive, Evaluation); grey ones get pushed down or out. Right: the reranked list. "Evaluation", which was last, jumps to fourth; near-duplicates like "Summary" and "Documents" are gone. The reranker can bring in words like "Metrics" and "Datasets" that were further down the original ranking. Same topic, better order.

So we design a new Lego block (Figure 5-20) that takes the first topic representation and outputs an improved one. In BERTopic these reranker models are called representation models.

Right brackets: the three stages, Clustering (topic creation), Representation (label topics), and the new Reranker (fine-tune representation). The arrow from step 3 to 4 skips nothing: only cluster labels go up. The new block 6 only sees the output of block 5.

Key benefit: the topic representation only needs to be optimised as many times as there are topics. With millions of documents and a hundred topics, the block runs once per topic, not once per document.

def topic_differences(model, original_topics, nr_topics=5):
    """Show the differences in topic representations between two models """
    df = pd.DataFrame(columns=["Topic", "Original", "Updated"])
    for topic in range(nr_topics):
        # Extract top 5 words per topic per model
        og_words = " | ".join(list(zip(*original_topics[topic]))[0][:5])
        new_words = " | ".join(list(zip(*model.get_topic(topic)))[0][:5])
        df.loc[len(df)] = [topic, og_words, new_words]
    return df
  • Each topic is a list of (word, weight) pairs. zip(*pairs) splits it into a tuple of words and a tuple of weights; [0] keeps the words; [:5] the top five.
  • The words are joined with " | " and added as one row per topic (topics 0 to 4 by default).
Picture it

An IRCTC Tatkal queue: a fast clerk first prints a shortlist of 30 candidate trains by simple filters (c-TF-IDF). Then a senior clerk, slower but smarter, reorders that shortlist by what you actually need (reranker). The senior clerk only looks at 30 trains, not all 13,000.

Where it breaks: a reranker can only reorder what the shortlist contains (KeyBERTInspired, MMR). The LLM block in 18 goes further and writes something new.

What's happening behind it

This is the two-stage retrieve-then-rerank pattern: a cheap, high-recall first stage narrows the options; an expensive, high-precision second stage reorders them. In BERTopic the expensive stage is cheap overall because its input count is the number of topics.

Interview angle

"Where else have you seen retrieve-then-rerank?" — "In RAG: BM25 or vector search returns top-50 chunks, a cross-encoder reranks them, and only the top 5 go to the LLM. BERTopic does the same with words: c-TF-IDF shortlists candidates, an embedding or LLM block reranks. The design rule is the same: put the expensive model where the input count is smallest."

16 KeyBERTInspired

pdf p180–181 · Fig 5-22

The first representation block is KeyBERTInspired, inspired by the keyword-extraction package KeyBERT (Grootendorst, 2020). KeyBERT extracts keywords by comparing word embeddings and document embeddings through cosine similarity.

BERTopic does something similar:

  1. Use c-TF-IDF to find the most representative documents per topic: the documents whose c-TF-IDF values are most similar to those of their topic.
  2. Embed those documents and take their average embedding per topic.
  3. Embed the candidate keywords (from c-TF-IDF) and compare them to that average.
  4. Rerank the keywords by that similarity.

How to read this figure. Purple = documents (each row an embedding), averaged into one purple vector for the topic. Grey = keyword embeddings. Cosine similarity of the purple vector against each grey row gives the bars on the right: cat is most similar to what the documents are about, cute next, that barely. A word like "that" might score well on counts but is meaningless semantically, so it drops.

Where it breaks: if your "vibe" model doesn't know a niche tag (#nmt), you'll drop a tag that experts would search for.

What's happening behind it

c-TF-IDF ranks by counts; KeyBERTInspired ranks by meaning: closeness of the keyword's embedding to the topic's centre. Stop words have embeddings far from any topical centre, so KeyBERTInspired removes nearly all of them (the book's note in 17). Abbreviations suffer because a small embedding model has seen "nmt" rarely, so its vector is unreliable.

Interview angle

"What's a failure mode of embedding-based keyword reranking?" — "It favours words the embedding model represents well, so domain abbreviations like NMT or EHR can be dropped even though experts rely on them, and it can return near-synonyms (translate, translating). I'd keep the c-TF-IDF keywords alongside, or add MMR for diversity."

17 Maximal marginal relevance (MMR)

pdf p181–182

With c-TF-IDF and KeyBERTInspired there's still significant redundancy: e.g. both "summaries" and "summary" in one topic, which are nearly the same.

Maximal marginal relevance (MMR) diversifies the representation. It looks for keywords that are diverse from one another but still related to the documents. It embeds a set of candidate keywords and iteratively picks the next best keyword to add. You set a diversity parameter: how different the keywords must be.

In BERTopic, MMR goes from an initial set of keywords, say 30, to a smaller but more diverse set, say 10. It filters out redundant words and keeps only words that add something new.

topic_model.update_topics(abstracts, representation_model=representation_model)

Book note

Both KeyBERTInspired and MMR are excellent for improving the first set of representations. KeyBERTInspired especially tends to remove nearly all stop words, since it focuses on semantic relationships between words and documents.

Try it: MMR picks 5 keywords for the summarization topic (modelled vectors)

Candidate words are the ten from Figure 5-19 with made-up embeddings. Score for each remaining word = (1 − diversity) × similarity to topic − diversity × (highest similarity to an already-picked word). The word with the best score is picked; repeat. Formula form is BERTopic's MMR (beyond the book); vectors and scores are modelled.

Worked example (modelled, the toy's own numbers)

"summarization" is picked first (most relevant, 0.94). Next candidates, with diversity 0.2: "summaries" has relevance 0.84 but similarity 0.97 to "summarization", so score = 0.8 × 0.84 − 0.2 × 0.97 ≈ 0.48. "document" has relevance 0.87 and similarity 0.78: 0.8 × 0.87 − 0.2 × 0.78 ≈ 0.54. "document" wins, and "summaries" keeps losing to less redundant words, so the final five hold only one summary-like word, as in the book's topic 4. Set diversity to 0 and "summaries" comes back. At diversity 0.5 "summaries" scores 0.5 × 0.84 − 0.5 × 0.97 ≈ −0.06: novelty now dominates. Diversity is a dial between "relevant" and "new".

Picture it

Picking a cricket XI. The best 11 batsmen by average would be a terrible team (all similar). MMR picks the best player, then each next pick is "good and different from who we already have": a keeper, a spinner, a fast bowler.

Where it breaks: a selector knows roles; MMR only knows embedding similarity, so two different words with similar vectors still count as redundant.

What's happening behind it

MMR is greedy: one pick at a time, each pick trading off relevance against the maximum similarity to what's already chosen. diversity = 0 is plain top-k by relevance; diversity = 1 ignores relevance and just maximises novelty. A small value like 0.2 removes near-duplicates without wandering off topic.

Interview angle

"Your RAG retriever returns five near-identical chunks. Fix?" — "Use MMR at retrieval: fetch top-30 by similarity, then greedily select 5 that are relevant but dissimilar to each other, with a diversity around 0.2–0.5. It's the same algorithm BERTopic uses to deduplicate topic keywords." LangChain and most vector stores offer an MMR search option (beyond the book).

18 The text generation Lego block

pdf p182–185 · Fig 5-23

So far the representation block has been a reranker. But, as Chapter 4 showed, generative models are useful for many tasks. We can use them in BERTopic efficiently by following part of the reranking idea: instead of asking a generative model for the topic of every document (possibly millions), we ask it to generate a label for each topic. Figure 5-23: instead of generating or reranking keywords, the model writes a short label from the keywords already found and a small set of representative documents.

How to read this figure. The blue LLM block is the sixth Lego block on the usual tower. Two inputs, keywords ("attention, text, nlp, transformer") and a few documents, are pasted into a prompt template at the bold placeholders [DOCUMENTS] and [KEYWORDS]. The model's answer is the green Label: "Pre-trained transformer models in NLP". A list of words became one human-readable name.

The two parts of the prompt:

  • [DOCUMENTS]: a small subset, typically four documents, that best represent the topic: those with the highest cosine similarity between their c-TF-IDF values and the topic's.
  • [KEYWORDS]: the topic's keywords, from c-TF-IDF or from any of the other representations (KeyBERTInspired, MMR…).

So the generative model runs once per topic (maybe hundreds) instead of once per document (maybe millions). Many generative models fit here, open source and proprietary. Start with Flan-T5 from Chapter 4:

  • TextGeneration(generator, prompt=...): BERTopic's wrapper that fills [DOCUMENTS] and [KEYWORDS] for each topic and calls the generator.
  • doc_length=50, tokenizer="whitespace": each representative document is cut to 50 words (split on whitespace) before going into the prompt, to keep prompts short for a small model.

Picture it

A wedding photographer's assistant groups 5,000 photos into albums (clustering) and tags each with words: "mehendi, hands, green" (c-TF-IDF). What's happening behind it

The LLM never sees all documents. It sees a compressed summary of the topic: top keywords (statistics over thousands of documents) plus four exemplars (concrete evidence), each cut to a fixed length. Keywords give breadth, exemplars give specificity. That's why GPT-3.5's labels can be very specific ("…Using HiPrBERT"), sometimes too specific: one representative abstract can dominate the label, which is another reason to keep keywords alongside.

Worked example (modelled)

Prompt size per topic ≈ 4 documents × 50 words + 10 keywords + ~40 template words ≈ 250 words ≈ 330 tokens. For 155 topics that's ≈ 51,000 input tokens in total, small change for any API. Labelling all 44,949 abstracts one by one at ~300 tokens each would be ≈ 13.5 million tokens, about 260× more.

Interview angle

"How would you use an LLM to make sense of 1M customer chats cheaply?" — "Don't send 1M chats to the LLM. Embed and cluster them, compute c-TF-IDF keywords, pick ~4 representative chats per cluster, and ask the LLM once per cluster for a short label with a fixed output format and backoff on rate limits. Keep keywords next to the labels because LLM labels can be too broad or overfit to one exemplar." That is exactly how you'd mine a voice agent's call logs for intents.

20 Summary and key terms

pdf p186–187

The chapter showed how LLMs, both generative and representative (embedding), work in unsupervised learning. Supervised methods like classification dominated recent years, but unsupervised approaches such as text clustering have great potential because they group texts by meaning without prior labels.

It covered a common clustering pipeline: convert text to embeddings, apply dimensionality reduction to simplify the high-dimensional data for better clustering, then run a clustering algorithm on the reduced embeddings. Manually inspecting the clusters showed which documents they held and how to interpret them.

To move beyond manual inspection, BERTopic extends the pipeline with an automatic way to represent clusters. This is topic modeling: uncovering themes in large amounts of documents. BERTopic builds the representations with a bag-of-words enhanced by c-TF-IDF, which weighs words by their relevance to a cluster and their frequency across all clusters.

A major benefit is BERTopic's modularity: any model in the pipeline can be chosen, which allows extra representations and multiple perspectives on the same topic. Maximal marginal relevance and KeyBERTInspired fine-tune the c-TF-IDF representations, and the same generative LLMs as Chapter 4 (Flan-T5 and GPT-3.5) generate highly interpretable labels.

22 Check yourself

click to reveal
1. What are the three steps of the common text-clustering pipeline, and which algorithm does the chapter use for each?

Embed (sentence-transformers thenlper/gte-small, 384 dims) → reduce dimensionality (UMAP to 5 dims) → cluster (HDBSCAN, min_cluster_size 50).

2. Why reduce dimensionality before clustering, and what is the cost?

In high dimensions the space of possible values grows exponentially and meaningful clusters get hard to find. Reduction compresses data while keeping global structure, but information is always lost; there's a balance between fewer dimensions and keeping information.

3. What do UMAP's n_components=5, min_dist=0.0, metric='cosine' and random_state=42 do?

5 output dimensions (5–10 generally works well); min distance 0 for tighter clusters; cosine because Euclidean methods struggle with high-dimensional data; reproducible results but no parallelism, so slower.

4. What would happen if you used k-means instead of HDBSCAN?

You'd have to choose the number of clusters in advance, and every document would be forced into a cluster: no outliers, no topic -1. Stray documents would pollute clusters.

5. HDBSCAN gave 156 clusters. How do you get more?

Lower min_cluster_size, which is the minimum size a cluster can have. (156 includes the -1 outlier label.)

6. Why shouldn't you trust the 2-D plot alone?

Any reduction for visualisation loses information; the plot can push clusters together or apart more than they really are. Human evaluation, reading the clusters, is a key part of cluster analysis.

7. How do classic topic models like LDA differ from embedding-based clustering?

LDA treats topics as probability distributions over the vocabulary and uses bag-of-words, ignoring context and meaning. Embedding-based clustering uses Transformer embeddings that capture context through attention.

8. What two problems of plain bag-of-words does c-TF-IDF solve?

(1) Bag-of-words is per document; c-TF counts words per cluster. (2) Stop words dominate; the IDF factor log(A/cfx + 1) gives less weight to words common across all clusters.

9. Using Figure 5-14's numbers, why does "cat" outrank "cute" in the purple cluster?

IDF(cat) = ln(29/1 + 1) ≈ 3.40, IDF(cute) = ln(29/22 + 1) ≈ 0.84. Weights: 1 × 3.40 = 3.40 vs 2 × 0.84 = 1.68. "cute" is common in other clusters, so it's down-weighted.

10. Why can a representation model be expensive per call yet cheap overall?

It runs once per topic, not once per document: e.g. a hundred topics instead of millions of documents.

11. KeyBERTInspired dropped "nmt" from the translation topic. Why, and is that good?

It ranks keywords by embedding similarity to the topic's average document embedding; the embedding model can't represent the abbreviation well. It's bad for domain experts, for whom abbreviations are highly informative.

12. What does MMR's diversity parameter control? What happens at 0?

How different the selected keywords must be from each other. At 0 it's just top-k by relevance (keeps "summary" and "summaries"); higher values remove redundancy, at the risk of drifting from the topic.

13. What goes into the [DOCUMENTS] and [KEYWORDS] tags of the TextGeneration prompt?

[DOCUMENTS]: typically four most representative documents, chosen by highest cosine similarity of their c-TF-IDF values to the topic's (cut to doc_length words). [KEYWORDS]: the topic's keywords from c-TF-IDF or another representation.

14. Interview: Flan-T5 labelled the medical topic "Science/Tech". What would you do?

Use a larger model (GPT-3.5 gave "Improved Representation Learning for Biomedical…"), improve the prompt with a fixed output format, and keep multiple representations (c-TF-IDF keywords, KeyBERTInspired, MMR) next to the label, since no model is perfect.

15. Interview: how do you remove the -1 topic without re-clustering?

BERTopic's reduce_outliers() reassigns outliers to existing topics. Alternatively, swap HDBSCAN for a non-outlier algorithm like k-means (but that does re-cluster).
