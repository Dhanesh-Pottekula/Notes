      How to use this page

      • Set your order. Drag a book by the dots on its left, use the arrows, or click its number and type a new position.
      • Sort. Choose a sort to look at the list another way. If you like it, press "Make this my order" and then adjust by hand.
      • Shortlist. Press "Reading" on a book to put it on the Reading now shelf at the top. Press "Read" when you finish it.
      • Order the shelf. The Reading now shelf has its own order: 1 is the book you are on, 2 is next. Drag a card by its dots, use its arrows, click its number to type a position, or use Arrange to sort the shelf in one step. A book you start reading joins at the end.
      • What each book teaches. The line under every title gives the philosophy behind the book and its core lesson. Open Details for three specific things it teaches.
      • Categories. Every book carries a category such as Philosophy or Philosophical fiction. Press a category to see only those books, or sort by Category to group the whole list.
      • Remove and add. "Remove" takes a book out of the list; you can restore it from Removed books. "Add a book" puts in one of your own.

      How the suggested order was decided

      Each of the original 50 books was scored from 1 to 5 on difficulty, using three things:

      • How dense the writing is. A novel you can read at speed scores low. A text where one page needs three readings scores high.
      • How much you need to know first. Aristotle and the later Dostoevsky assume a lot. Kafka assumes nothing.
      • Length and stamina. A long book is harder to finish, even when each page is easy.

      The books then run from low to high, with three adjustments. Novels and non-fiction alternate where possible, so that dense reading is followed by a story. A few books sit next to each other on purpose, because one answers the other: Camus' essay is followed by the novel that acts it out. And the novels of stage 4 come after the philosophy of stage 3 even though several are easier to read: they are harder to interpret, and they dramatise the ideas you will have just met.

      The difficulty scores and the suggested order are a judgement, not a measurement. Ratings measure how much readers enjoyed a book, not how good or how hard it is.

      How to read the philosophy books

      Do not read them like novels. For every important chapter, write down five answers:

      1. What question is the author trying to answer?
      2. What is the main argument, in two or three sentences?
      3. What does the author assume without proving?
      4. Do I agree? Why, or why not?
      5. Can I think of a real example that supports it, and one that breaks it?
