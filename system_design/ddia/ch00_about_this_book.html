Martin Kleppmann · Designing Data-Intensive Applications · Chapter 0 (Preface) · PDF p15–20

Why this book exists, who it is for, and the road map of all 11 chapters

The preface opens with the buzzwords every backend engineer hears: NoSQL, Big Data, CAP, MapReduce and more. It explains where they came from: huge internet companies, the need to move fast, open source, multi-core CPUs (processors), rented cloud machines and the demand for zero downtime. It then names the subject: a data-intensive application is one where the hard part is the data (how much, how complex, how fast it changes), not the CPU. Its promise is simple: skip the hype and learn the lasting principles and trade-offs behind real systems, so you can pick the right tool and fix it when it misbehaves. It says who should read it (backend people who know SQL, the Structured Query Language of relational databases) and what it leaves out (install guides, operations, security, ethics…). Last, it lays out three parts: one machine (Ch.1–4), many machines (Ch.5–9), and many different systems glued together (Ch.10–11, plus a final chapter not yet written in this early release).

Six forces that changed how we build data systems

pdf p15

The book lists the "driving forces" behind the last decade's developments. All six, in the book's order:

  1. Huge internet companies. Google, Yahoo!, Amazon, Facebook, LinkedIn and Twitter handle huge volumes of data and traffic. That forced them to create new tools to handle such scale efficiently.
  2. Businesses must be agile. They need to test ideas (hypotheses) cheaply and react quickly to new market insights. That means short development cycles and flexible data models.
  3. Free and open source software (FOSS) won. It became very successful and is now preferred over commercial or bespoke (custom-built, in-house) software in many places.
  4. CPUs stopped getting much faster; they got more cores. CPU clock speeds are barely increasing, but multi-core processors are standard and networks are getting faster. So parallelism (doing many things at once) is only going to increase.
  5. Even small teams can go distributed. Thanks to IaaS (infrastructure as a service), meaning you rent machines instead of buying them, such as Amazon Web Services (AWS), a small team can run systems spread across many machines and even multiple geographic regions.
  6. High availability is expected. Many services are expected to be always up. Long downtime, whether from outages or from planned maintenance, is becoming more and more unacceptable.

Step through the six forces

Picture it

Think of how the Indian Railways reservation system had to change. Once, you queued at a counter and a clerk typed into one central computer. Then came IRCTC on the web: millions of people hit "Book" at 10:00 AM for Tatkal tickets (force 1: huge traffic). Rules and quotas change often (force 2: agility). Phones in every pocket mean the site can never be "closed for maintenance" at festival time (force 6). The old single-counter model could not survive these pressures, so the systems behind it had to be rebuilt in a new way.

Where it breaks: this is an analogy for the kind of pressure, not a statement about how IRCTC is actually built; the book doesn't discuss IRCTC.

What's happening behind it

Force 4 is the quiet but deep one. If one CPU core can't get faster, the only way to do more work is to split it across cores, and then across machines. The moment work is split across machines, you get the hard problems of Part II of the book: copies that disagree, networks that drop messages, clocks that differ. Force 5 (cloud) makes that situation normal even for a team of three. Force 6 (no downtime) means you can't solve those problems by simply stopping the system.

Worked example (modelled numbers, not from the book)

Your job takes 8 hours on one core. Clock speeds are flat, so a new CPU won't make it 2× faster. But a 16-core machine can, in the ideal case, finish in 8 ÷ 16 = 30 minutes, if the work can be split into 16 independent pieces. Ten such machines rented from a cloud provider could, ideally, take it to about 3 minutes. The catch: splitting and combining the work, and coping when one of those machines fails halfway, is exactly what Ch.8–10 are about.

Try it: split the 8-hour job yourself (modelled)

Modelled numbers, not from the book. Assumptions: the job is 480 minutes of work on one core and splits perfectly. Each extra machine adds 1 minute of cost to split the work and combine the results. Each machine has a 2% chance per hour of failing during the run.

What to notice: going from 1 to 16 cores is a huge win (force 4). Adding machines (force 5) helps less and less, because splitting and combining has a cost, and the chance that some machine fails during the run goes up. That trade-off is why Part II of the book exists.

What "data-intensive" means

pdf p15–16

Data-intensive applications push the limits of what is possible by using the developments above. The book's definition:

An application is DATA-INTENSIVE if data is its primary challenge: • the QUANTITY of data (how much) • the COMPLEXITY of data (how tangled / connected) • the SPEED at which it changes (how fast) as opposed to COMPUTE-INTENSIVE, where CPU cycles are the bottleneck. (CPU = central processing unit, the chip that does the calculating.)

The tools that help such applications store and process data have been adapting fast. New kinds of databases ("NoSQL") got most of the attention, but the book stresses that other building blocks matter just as much:

  • message queues (hand data from one process to another, asynchronously),
  • caches (keep results of expensive work so the next read is fast),
  • search indexes (find records by keyword or filter),
  • frameworks for batch and stream processing (crunch large datasets all at once, or continuously as events arrive),
  • and related technologies.

Many applications use some combination of these. (The short descriptions in brackets are plain-word hints; the book explains each one properly in Ch.1 and later chapters.)

Picture it

Compute-intensive is a chess grandmaster: one board, few pieces, but enormous thinking per move. Data-intensive is a big Mumbai dabbawala network: each step is simple (pick up, sort, deliver), but there are lakhs of tiffins, routes are tangled, and everything changes every minute. The difficulty is in the volume, the connections and the speed, not in any single hard calculation.

Where it breaks: dabbawalas almost never "lose" a tiffin; real data systems must expect lost messages and crashed machines and are designed around that.

No single product does all of this well, which is why apps stitch several tools together.

Principles over buzzwords: the book's goal

pdf p16

The buzzwords are a sign of enthusiasm for new possibilities, and the book calls that a great thing. But as engineers and architects we need a technically accurate and precise understanding of the technologies and their trade-offs to build good applications. For that, we must "dig deeper than buzzwords".

The good news: behind the fast-changing tools there are enduring principles that stay true no matter which version of a tool you use. If you understand them, you can see:

  • where each tool fits in,
  • how to make good use of it,
  • how to avoid its pitfalls.

The goal of the book: help you navigate the diverse, fast-changing landscape of technologies for storing and processing data.

What it is NOT

  • Not a tutorial for one particular tool.
  • Not a textbook full of dry theory.

What it IS

  • A look at successful data systems: technologies that are the foundation of many popular apps and must meet scalability, performance and reliability requirements in production every day.
  • A dig into their internals: key algorithms, principles, and the trade-offs they make.

On this journey the book tries to find useful ways of thinking about data systems: not just how they work, but why they work that way, and what questions we need to ask.

What you will (and won't) be able to do after reading

  • ✔ Decide which kind of technology fits which purpose.
  • ✔ Understand how tools can be combined to form the foundation of a good application architecture.
  • ✘ You won't be ready to build your own database storage engine from scratch, but the book notes that is rarely necessary.
  • ✔ You will develop a good intuition for what your systems are doing under the hood, so you can reason about their behaviour, make good design decisions, and track down problems that come up.

The three layers of understanding

The book aims at the bottom layer; knowing it lets you judge the two layers above.

Worked example: meeting a brand-new tool

Your team lead says: "We're adding database X. It's a distributed key-value store." You have never seen X. A buzzword learner asks: "Is it web-scale?" A principle learner asks the questions the book trains you to ask:

  1. How does it store data on one disk? (Ch.3)
  2. Does it keep copies? From a leader, or from every node? (Ch.5)
  3. How does it split data across machines? (Ch.6)
  4. What happens if two people write the same key at once? (Ch.7)
  5. What does it do when the network between machines breaks? (Ch.8–9)

Five questions, and you can predict how X will behave on a bad day, before reading its manual. That is "what questions we need to ask" from the preface, made concrete.

Picture it

Learning cricket by memorising today's IPL team lists is the buzzword layer: useless next season. Learning why a captain puts a slip fielder for a swing bowler, or why you bat defensively when chasing a small target on a turning pitch, is the principle layer: it works for every team, every year, even for a format that doesn't exist yet.

Where it breaks: cricket rules rarely change; data tools do change in big ways, and occasionally a new tool really does change which trade-offs are possible.

What's happening behind it

Most data tools are different mixes of a small set of ideas: logs, indexes, copies, partitions, ordering, consensus. Once you can spot which ideas a tool uses, you can predict its strengths and failure modes without having used it. That is the "intuition under the hood" the preface promises.

Who should read this book?

The core audience: if you build applications with some kind of server/backend for storing or processing data, and your apps use the internet (for example web apps, mobile apps, or internet-connected sensors), the book is for you.

It is written for software engineers, software architects and technical managers who love to code. "You're not Google or Amazon…"

People sometimes say: "you're not Google or Amazon, stop worrying about scale and just use a relational database." The book's answer has two halves:

The true half

Building for scale you don't need is wasted effort and may lock you into an inflexible design. It is a form of premature optimization.

The other half

It's still important to choose the right tool for the job; different technologies each have their own strengths and weaknesses. Relational databases are important, but not the final word on dealing with data.

Picture it

Opening a small chai stall? Don't build a factory kitchen with conveyor belts "in case you become Chaayos". That's premature optimization: money gone, and the layout is stuck. But that doesn't mean "always use one big kettle for everything". If half your orders are cold coffee, you need a fridge and a blender, not a bigger kettle. Right tool, right size.

Where it breaks: a stall can buy a blender in an afternoon; changing the database of a running app is much harder, which is why the choice deserves real thought early.

What's happening behind it

Scale is one requirement among many. A relational DB may handle your load easily but still be a poor fit if, say, your data is a highly connected graph, or you need full-text search, or you need to react to events within milliseconds. The book wants you to choose based on the shape of the problem, not just its size.

06 · SECTION

Scope of this book

pdf p17–18

No install guides. The book does not give detailed instructions for installing or using specific software packages or APIs (application programming interfaces: the functions or endpoints a product gives you to call); plenty of documentation exists for that. Instead it discusses the principles and trade-offs that are fundamental to data systems, and explores the different design decisions different products have taken.

Not new ideas, but a summary of them. Most of what the book discusses has already been said somewhere: conference talks, research papers, blog posts, code, bug trackers, and "engineering folklore". The book gathers the most important ideas from many sources and gives pointers to the original literature throughout. The references at the end of each chapter are a great resource for going deeper.

Focus: architecture. The book looks mainly at the architecture of data systems and how they are integrated into data-intensive applications.

In scope

  • Principles and trade-offs of data systems
  • Design decisions of different products
  • Internals: algorithms, storage, replication, etc.
  • How systems are integrated into applications

Out of scope (deserve books of their own)

  • Installing / using specific packages or APIs
  • Deployment
  • Operations
  • Security
  • Ethics
  • Management

The book's reason for leaving these out: they are complex and important, and it "wouldn't do them justice" as superficial side-notes.

Why the book avoids the word "Big Data"

Many technologies in the book fall under the Big Data buzzword. But the term is so over-used and under-defined that it is not useful in a serious engineering discussion. The book uses less ambiguous terms instead, along two axes:

  • single-node vs. distributed systems (one machine vs. many machines),
  • online/interactive vs. offline/batch processing systems (answers a user who is waiting vs. crunches a big job in the background).

Bias toward free and open source

The book leans toward FOSS (free and open source software) because reading, modifying and running source code is a great way to understand in detail how something works. Open platforms also reduce the risk of vendor lock-in (being stuck with one company's product because moving away is too costly). But where it makes sense, the book also covers proprietary software, which it splits into three kinds: closed-source software, software as a service (SaaS), and companies' in-house software that is only described in published literature but never released.

Picture it

"Big Data" is like saying "big vehicle". A Volvo bus, a goods train and an oil tanker are all "big vehicles", but that tells a mechanic nothing. Better to ask: Does it run on one engine or many coupled units? (single-node vs distributed) and Does it carry passengers who are waiting right now, or freight that just needs to arrive by tomorrow? (online vs batch). Those two questions tell you how to design it.

Where it breaks: real systems often sit in more than one box (a database can serve users online and run nightly batch reports), and later chapters show stream processing sitting between online and batch.

What's happening behind it

The two axes predict the hard problems. Going distributed brings network failures, replica disagreement and the need for consensus (Part II). Going online means latency matters and a user is waiting; going batch means throughput matters and the job can be re-run if it fails (Ch.10). "Big" says nothing about either.

Picture it

Learning to run a restaurant kitchen. Part I: one cook, one kitchen: how to store ingredients so you find them fast (storage), how to write recipes others can read (encoding). Part II: a chain with branches in five cities: keeping menus in sync (replication), splitting the city by delivery zones (partitioning), making sure an order is either fully placed or not at all (transactions), and what happens when the phone line between branches drops (distributed trouble, consensus). Part III: the whole food business: the central kitchen preps sauces in big nightly batches (batch), and the Swiggy/Zomato order stream flows in live all day (stream).

Where it breaks: restaurant branches can phone each other and sort things out with common sense; computers can't, so they need exact protocols (Ch.9).

What's happening behind it

Each part depends on the one before. You can't reason about copying data across machines (Part II) until you know how one machine stores and encodes it (Part I). And you can't safely glue many systems together (Part III) until you understand what can go wrong between machines (Part II). That's why the book climbs in this order.

Summary (in plain words)

pdf p15–19
  • Backend engineers face a flood of data buzzwords. They come from real changes: giant internet companies, business agility, open source, multi-core CPUs and faster networks, cheap cloud machines (IaaS), and demand for high availability.
  • A data-intensive app is one whose main challenge is data: its quantity, complexity, or speed of change. A compute-intensive app is limited by CPU instead.
  • Such apps combine many tools: databases (including NoSQL), message queues, caches, search indexes, batch and stream processing frameworks.
  • Tools change fast; principles and trade-offs last. The book studies real, successful systems from the inside to teach those principles: how, why, and what questions to ask.
  • You'll learn to choose and combine tools and to reason about and debug them; you won't learn to write a storage engine from scratch (rarely needed).
  • Audience: engineers, architects and coding managers who build internet-connected backends; you should know web/network services and relational databases + SQL; TCP/HTTP helps; language doesn't matter.
  • "You're not Google" is half right: don't build for scale you don't need (premature optimization), but do pick the right tool; relational DBs aren't the final word.
  • Scope: principles and design decisions, not installation guides; architecture, not deployment, operations, security, ethics or management. Ideas are gathered from many sources, with references at the end of every chapter.
  • "Big Data" is too vague; the book says single-node vs distributed and online/interactive vs offline/batch instead. It favours open source but covers proprietary systems where useful.
  • Three parts: Part I one machine (Ch.1–4), Part II many machines (Ch.5–9), Part III many different systems (Ch.10–11, plus an unwritten final chapter "???").
  • It's an early release; send feedback to feedback@dataintensive.net.

Key terms

TermPlain meaning
Data-intensive applicationAn app where data (how much, how complex, how fast it changes) is the main challenge.
Compute-intensive applicationAn app where CPU cycles are the bottleneck.
Backend / server-sideThe part of a system that runs on servers and stores or processes data, behind the user's screen.
NoSQLThe umbrella name for newer, non-relational kinds of databases (explained in Ch.2).
Distributed systemSoftware running on several machines that cooperate over a network.
ParallelismDoing many pieces of work at the same time (on many cores or machines).
IaaS (infrastructure as a service)Renting machines, storage and networks from a provider like Amazon Web Services instead of owning them.
High availabilityThe service stays up; little or no downtime from outages or maintenance.
FOSSFree and open source software: code you can read, change and run.
Proprietary softwareClosed-source products, SaaS (software as a service), or in-house systems only described in papers.
Vendor lock-inBeing stuck with one supplier because leaving is too hard or costly.
Premature optimizationSpending effort to optimize for a need (like huge scale) you don't have yet, often making the design rigid.
Single-node vs distributedRuns on one machine vs runs across many machines.
Online/interactive vs offline/batchAnswers a waiting user quickly vs processes a large job in the background.
Heterogeneous systemA system made of several different kinds of components (DBs, caches, indexes…).
Serialization (encoding)Turning in-memory data into bytes to store or send (Ch.4).
Replication / Partitioning (sharding)Keeping copies on several machines / splitting data into pieces across machines (Ch.5 / Ch.6).
Log (append-only)A file you only ever add to at the end; old entries are never changed in place (this page's example in plate 4; Ch.3, Ch.11).
IndexA separate lookup structure that says where to find a record, so you don't scan everything (Ch.3).
SQL / API / TCP / HTTPStructured Query Language; application programming interface; Transmission Control Protocol; HyperText Transfer Protocol.

Beyond the book: where this shows up in your own work

As a backend engineer, a typical stack (for example PostgreSQL for records, Redis as a cache, Elasticsearch for search, Kafka or a queue for events, a nightly job for reports) is exactly the "combination of tools" the preface describes. These product names are not in the preface; they are examples to anchor the idea, and the book discusses many of them in later chapters.

Check yourself

11 questions
1. What three properties of data make an application "data-intensive"?

The quantity of data, its complexity, and the speed at which it changes. If instead CPU cycles are the bottleneck, it's compute-intensive.

2. Name the six driving forces the preface lists.

(1) Internet giants handling huge data/traffic built new tools; (2) businesses need agility: cheap hypothesis tests, short dev cycles, flexible data models; (3) free and open source software became preferred; (4) clock speeds flat, multi-core standard, faster networks, so more parallelism; (5) IaaS such as AWS lets small teams build distributed, multi-region systems; (6) high availability is expected; downtime is increasingly unacceptable.

3. Besides NoSQL databases, which building blocks does the preface say are "very important too"?

Message queues, caches, search indexes, frameworks for batch and stream processing, and related technologies. Many apps combine several of them.

4. Why does the book focus on principles instead of specific tools?

Tools change fast, but the principles behind them stay true regardless of tool version. Knowing them shows where each tool fits, how to use it well, and how to avoid its pitfalls.

5. After reading, will you be able to build your own storage engine from scratch?

No, the book says you won't be ready for that (and it's rarely needed). You will get a good intuition for what systems do under the hood, so you can reason about them, design well and debug problems.

6. What prior knowledge does the book expect, and what doesn't matter?

Expected: some experience building web apps or network services, and familiarity with relational databases and SQL. Helpful: general TCP/HTTP knowledge. Bonus but not required: non-relational DBs and other data tools. Doesn't matter: your programming language or framework.

7. A colleague says "we're not Google, just use Postgres for everything". What would the book say?

Partly right: building for scale you don't need is wasted effort, may lock you into an inflexible design, and is a form of premature optimization. But you still need the right tool for the job; each technology has strengths and weaknesses, and relational DBs are important but not the final word.

8. Which five areas does the book explicitly leave out, and why?

Deployment, operations, security, ethics and management. They're complex and important and would not get justice as superficial side-notes; they deserve their own books. (It also skips install/API instructions, since documentation already covers those.)

9. Why doesn't the book use the term "Big Data", and what does it use instead?

It's over-used and under-defined, so not useful in serious engineering discussion. Instead: single-node vs distributed systems, and online/interactive vs offline/batch processing systems.

10. Why the bias toward open source, and what kinds of proprietary software are still covered?

Reading, modifying and running source code is a great way to understand how something works, and open platforms reduce vendor lock-in. Still covered where appropriate: closed-source software, SaaS, and companies' in-house software described only in literature.

11. What if you skipped Part I and jumped straight to Ch.9 consensus? What would be missing?

Part II builds on Part I: you need to know how one machine stores and encodes data (Ch.3–4) before reasoning about copies and partitions across machines (Ch.5–6), and Ch.8's failure modes before Ch.9's consistency and consensus make sense. The preface lays the parts out in exactly this dependency order.
