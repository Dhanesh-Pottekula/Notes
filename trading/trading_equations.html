Measure a strategy

Before asking "does it make money", ask "how much per trade, per unit of risk, and how bad can it get". Put these in every backtest report and compare against simply holding the Nifty.

1 · Expectancy average result per trade

Learn first

How much you make on an average trade, measured in R (the amount you risk per trade). Positive expectancy after costs is the minimum requirement for any strategy.

\[ E = p\,W - (1-p)\,L \]
p
win rate (fraction of trades that win)
W, L
average win and average loss, both in R or ₹
Example Daily trendline-break setup: win rate 51.5%, target 2R, stop 1R.
E = 0.515 × 2 − 0.485 × 1 = 0.545R per trade. Risking ₹10,000 per trade, that is about ₹5,450 per trade before brokerage, STT and slippage.
Watch outSubtract costs per trade before judging. On short timeframes the charges can be bigger than the whole edge.

2 · Sharpe ratio return per unit of volatility

Learn first

The standard way to compare strategies with different risk. Above 1 (annualised) is good for a single strategy; buy-and-hold Nifty is usually somewhere around 0.4–0.8 depending on the period.

\[ S = \frac{\bar R_p - R_f}{\sigma_p} \qquad S_{\text{annual}} = S_{\text{daily}} \times \sqrt{252} \]
R̄ₚ
average return of the strategy per period
R_f
risk-free return for the same period (e.g. 91-day T-bill ÷ 252 for daily)
σₚ
standard deviation of the strategy's returns
252
trading days in a year (use √52 for weekly, √12 for monthly)
Example Daily mean return 0.06%, daily std 1.2%, risk-free 6.5% a year (0.0258% a day).
(0.0006 − 0.000258) ÷ 0.012 × √252 ≈ 0.45 annualised. Roughly index-like, so not worth the effort unless it's uncorrelated with the index.
Watch outSharpe treats upside and downside swings the same, and it looks great for strategies that sell options until the day they blow up. Always read it next to max drawdown.

3 · Sortino ratio Sharpe that only punishes losses

Useful next
\[ \text{Sortino} = \frac{\bar R_p - R_f}{\sigma_{\text{down}}}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{down}} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum \min(R_t - R_f,\,0)^2} \]
σ_down
downside deviation: only periods below the risk-free rate count
Watch outTrend-following strategies with big occasional winners look much better on Sortino than on Sharpe. That's fair, but check the number of trades: a few lucky outliers can carry it.

4 · Maximum drawdown worst peak-to-trough fall

Learn first

The number that decides whether you could actually stick with a strategy in real life.

\[ \text{MDD} = \max_t \frac{\text{Peak}_t - \text{Equity}_t}{\text{Peak}_t} \qquad \text{gain to recover} = \frac{1}{1-\text{MDD}} - 1 \]
Peak_t
highest equity value reached up to time t
Example Account grows ₹10L → ₹14L, then falls to ₹11.2L. MDD = (14 − 11.2) ÷ 14 = 20%. Getting back to ₹14L needs 1 ÷ 0.8 − 1 = 25%. A 50% drawdown needs +100%.
Watch outYour live drawdown will usually be worse than the backtest's. Plan for about 1.5–2× the backtested figure.

5 · CAPM beta and alpha how much is just the market

Useful next

Beta is how much your strategy moves with the Nifty. Alpha is the return left over after paying for that market exposure. Fama–French adds size and value factors on top.

\[ \beta = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\operatorname{Var}(R_m)} \qquad \alpha = R_i - \big[R_f + \beta\,(R_m - R_f)\big] \]
\[ R_i - R_f = \alpha + \beta_m (R_m - R_f) + \beta_s\,\text{SMB} + \beta_v\,\text{HML} + \varepsilon \quad\text{(Fama–French 3-factor)} \]
R_m
market (Nifty 50) return
SMB
small-minus-big: return of small caps over large caps
HML
high-minus-low: return of value stocks over growth stocks
Example Strategy made 18%, Nifty made 12%, risk-free 6.5%, beta 1.2.
Fair return for that risk = 6.5 + 1.2 × (12 − 6.5) = 13.1%, so α = 18 − 13.1 = 4.9%. Only that 4.9% is skill.
Watch outA strategy with high beta beats the index in bull markets for free. Check alpha, not raw return.

Size positions

Most blown accounts had a fine entry signal and the wrong position size. These four decide how much to put on each trade.

6 · Kelly criterion growth-optimal bet size

Learn first

The fraction of capital to risk per trade that maximises long-run growth, given your win rate and payoff. Everyone in practice uses a quarter to a half of it.

\[ f^{*} = p - \frac{1-p}{b} \]
p
win rate
b
reward-to-risk ratio (average win ÷ average loss)
f*
fraction of capital to risk (lose if stopped out), not position size
Example Win rate 51.5%, 2R target: f* = 0.515 − 0.485 ÷ 2 = 27.3% of capital at risk per trade.
Half Kelly 13.6%, quarter Kelly 6.8%. Both are still far above the 1–2% most traders risk, because a backtested win rate is only an estimate.
Watch outIf your true win rate is a few points lower than the backtest says, full Kelly over-bets badly and drawdowns get brutal. Betting too much hurts far more than betting too little.

7 · Merton fraction how much of your money in stocks

Useful next

The continuous-time version of Kelly, from Merton's portfolio problem. With γ = 1 it is exactly Kelly.

\[ \pi^{*} = \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma\,\sigma^{2}} \]
μ − r
expected return above the risk-free rate
σ
annual volatility
γ
risk aversion (1 = Kelly; 2–5 is typical for real people)
Example Nifty: assume μ = 12%, r = 6.5%, σ = 16%.
γ = 1: 0.055 ÷ 0.0256 = 215% (2.15× leverage, which shows why full Kelly is too aggressive). γ = 4: 54% in equity, rest in debt.
Watch outThe answer is very sensitive to μ, the number you know least. A 2-point error in expected return changes the allocation a lot.

8 · Fixed-fractional position size shares to buy for a given stop

Learn first

Turns "risk 1% of my account" into an exact quantity.

\[ \text{Qty} = \frac{\text{Equity} \times \text{risk}\%}{\lvert\,\text{Entry} - \text{Stop}\,\rvert} \]
Example ₹10,00,000 account, risk 1% (₹10,000). Buy at ₹1,450, stop at ₹1,420 (₹30 risk per share).
Qty = 10,000 ÷ 30 = 333 shares ≈ ₹4.83L of stock. A tighter stop means more shares for the same rupee risk.
Watch outGaps jump over stops. Overnight and F&O positions can lose more than the planned risk.

9 · Volatility targeting fractional Kelly the professional way

Useful next

Scale each position so it contributes the same volatility. Trend-following funds do this; it behaves like a fractional-Kelly rule.

\[ w = \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\hat\sigma_{\text{asset}}} \]
Example Target 15% annual volatility per position. A stock running at 30% gets w = 0.15 ÷ 0.30 = 50% weight; a calm one at 12% gets 125% (capped at 100% without leverage).

Volatility

Volatility is the one thing in markets that is reasonably forecastable: calm follows calm and storms follow storms.

10 · Average True Range volatility in rupees

Learn first

The volatility measure inside SuperTrend and most stop-loss rules.

\[ TR_t = \max\big(H_t - L_t,\ \lvert H_t - C_{t-1}\rvert,\ \lvert L_t - C_{t-1}\rvert\big) \qquad ATR_t = \frac{(n-1)\,ATR_{t-1} + TR_t}{n} \]
H, L, C
high, low, close; C₍t−1₎ is yesterday's close (captures gaps)
n
lookback, usually 14 (Wilder's smoothing)
Example ATR(14) = ₹28 on a ₹1,450 stock. A 2-ATR stop sits ₹56 below entry; with ₹10,000 risk that's 178 shares.

11 · Annualised volatility the square-root-of-time rule

Learn first
\[ \sigma_{\text{annual}} = \sigma_{\text{daily}}\sqrt{252} \qquad \text{expected 1-day move} \approx P \times \sigma_{\text{daily}} \]
Example Daily std of returns 1.5% → 1.5% × √252 ≈ 23.8% a year. On a ₹1,450 stock a typical day moves about ±₹22.
Watch outReturns have fat tails. A "4-sigma" day happens several times a decade, not once in a lifetime.

12 · GARCH(1,1) volatility forecasting

Useful next

Today's variance = a long-run base + a reaction to yesterday's shock + memory of yesterday's variance. Used for forecasting volatility and for sizing.

\[ \sigma_t^{2} = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^{2} + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^{2} \qquad \bar\sigma^{2} = \frac{\omega}{1-\alpha-\beta} \]
ε₍t−1₎
yesterday's surprise return
α
reaction to news (equity indices typically ≈ 0.05–0.10)
β
persistence (typically ≈ 0.85–0.93); α + β must be below 1
σ̄²
long-run variance the forecast drifts back to
Watch outFit it on returns, not prices. In Python, the arch package does this in a few lines.

Options

Only needed if you trade F&O. Every option chain's implied volatility and Greeks come from these.

13 · Black–Scholes–Merton European option price and delta

Useful next

Traders use it backwards: plug in the market price and solve for σ. That σ is the implied volatility shown on the chain. The model assumes constant volatility, which the real smile contradicts.

\[ C = S\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2) \qquad P = K e^{-rT} N(-d_2) - S\,N(-d_1) \]
\[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2})T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} \qquad \Delta_C = N(d_1),\ \ \Delta_P = N(d_1) - 1 \]
\[ C - P = S - K e^{-rT} \quad\text{(put–call parity)} \]
S, K
spot price, strike price
T
time to expiry in years (days ÷ 365)
r, σ
risk-free rate, volatility (annual)
N(·)
standard normal cumulative distribution
Example Nifty at 25,000, ATM 25,000 strike, 30 days, r = 6.5%, σ = 14%.
d₁ = 0.153, d₂ = 0.113 → Call ≈ ₹469, Put ≈ ₹336, call delta 0.56, put delta −0.44. Parity check: 469 − 336 = 133 = 25,000 − 25,000·e^(−0.065×30/365).
Watch outIndex options are European, but the Nifty has a skew: out-of-the-money puts trade at higher IV than calls. One σ for all strikes misprices the wings.

14 · Expected move from implied volatility

Useful next
\[ \text{1σ move} \approx S \times \sigma_{\text{IV}} \times \sqrt{\tfrac{\text{days}}{365}} \]
Example Nifty 25,000, IV 14%, 30 days: 25,000 × 0.14 × √(30/365) ≈ ±1,003 points. The market prices about a 68% chance of finishing between roughly 24,000 and 26,000.

Mean reversion and pairs

A different kind of edge from indicator signals: trade the gap between two related stocks (HDFC Bank vs ICICI Bank, say) instead of predicting direction.

15 · Spread and z-score

Useful next
\[ s_t = \ln A_t - \beta \ln B_t \qquad z_t = \frac{s_t - \mu_s}{\sigma_s} \]
β
hedge ratio from regressing ln A on ln B
μ_s, σ_s
rolling mean and std of the spread
Rule of thumb Short the spread when z > +2, buy it when z < −2, exit near z = 0. First check the pair is cointegrated (Engle–Granger test, p < 0.05); two stocks that merely move together are not enough.

16 · Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process how fast the gap closes

Useful next
\[ dX_t = \theta(\mu - X_t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t \qquad \text{half-life} = \frac{\ln 2}{\theta} \]

To estimate θ, regress the daily change in the spread on yesterday's spread: Δsₜ = a + b·sₜ₋₁, then θ = −b.

Example Regression gives b = −0.07 per day → half-life = ln 2 ÷ 0.07 ≈ 9.9 days. Set your time stop around 2–3 half-lives; if the gap hasn't closed by then, the relationship may have broken.

Regimes

The Jim Simons angle: the market has hidden states you can't see directly, only infer from prices.

17 · Hidden Markov model forward algorithm

Know it exists

Fit a 2- or 3-state HMM to daily returns (calm / volatile, trending / choppy) and use the current state as a filter: only take your trend strategy in the trending state.

\[ \alpha_t(j) = \Big[\sum_i \alpha_{t-1}(i)\,a_{ij}\Big]\, b_j(o_t) \qquad P(\text{state}_t = j \mid o_{1..t}) = \frac{\alpha_t(j)}{\sum_k \alpha_t(k)} \]
a_ij
probability of moving from state i to state j
b_j(o_t)
how likely today's return is in state j (e.g. a normal with that state's mean and volatility)
Watch outUse only the filtered probability (data up to today). The smoothed one peeks at the future and makes backtests look far too good. Python: hmmlearn. Books: Bishop ch.13, AIMA ch.14.

Institutional models

Worth understanding, but they need tick data and fast execution, so they're rarely tradable for an individual.

18 · Almgren–Chriss optimal execution of a large order

Know it exists

Sell X shares over time T while balancing market impact (selling fast is expensive) against price risk (selling slowly leaves you exposed).

\[ \min\ \mathbb{E}[\text{cost}] + \lambda\,\operatorname{Var}[\text{cost}] \quad\Rightarrow\quad x_t = X\,\frac{\sinh\!\big(\kappa (T - t)\big)}{\sinh(\kappa T)} \]
x_t
shares still to sell at time t
κ
urgency: grows with risk aversion λ and volatility, shrinks with impact cost

19 · Avellaneda–Stoikov market-maker quotes

Know it exists

A market maker shifts both quotes away from the inventory it is holding, so it gets pushed back toward flat.

\[ r = s - q\,\gamma\,\sigma^{2}(T - t) \qquad \delta^{a} + \delta^{b} = \gamma\,\sigma^{2}(T - t) + \frac{2}{\gamma}\ln\!\Big(1 + \frac{\gamma}{k}\Big) \]
r, s
reservation (fair-to-me) price, mid price
q
current inventory (long is positive)
δᵃ + δᵇ
total bid–ask spread to quote
k
how quickly fill probability drops as quotes move away from mid
