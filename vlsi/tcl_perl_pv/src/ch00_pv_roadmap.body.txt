title: The whole physical verification roadmap: from running checks to owning sign-off
kicker: The big picture · about 10 months beside a full-time job
lede: Scripting is one row of the skill table. This page is the whole table: what a physical verification (PV) engineer has to know, in what order to learn it, how to practise each part, and what to read. It is written for someone with one to three years of experience who runs DRC and LVS in Calibre today and wants to be trusted with sign-off for a chip at a product company.
---
<div class="box warn"><strong>How far to trust this page.</strong> The concepts here are standard and are the same at every foundry and in every tool. The numbers, rule names and switches are not: they live in your foundry's design rule manual and your tool's manuals, which are confidential and differ per process. Where this page gives an example value, it is only an illustration. Nothing on this page was run in a tool: it is concepts, written from memory and from the articles linked. Each resource link was checked only for a successful response when this page was written, and I did not read every one in full.</div>

<h2 id="job">What the job really is</h2>
<p>A foundry will build exactly the shapes you send. PV is the last proof that those shapes can be manufactured, that they form the circuit the designers meant, and that the circuit will survive manufacture and years of use. Each check answers one question:</p>
<div class="tablewrap"><table>
<tr><th>Check</th><th>Question it answers</th><th>What it compares</th></tr>
<tr><td>DRC (design rule check)</td><td>Can the fab build these shapes with good yield?</td><td>layout shapes against the foundry's geometric rules</td></tr>
<tr><td>LVS (layout versus schematic)</td><td>Is this layout the same circuit as the netlist?</td><td>devices and nets extracted from layout against the source netlist</td></tr>
<tr><td>Antenna</td><td>Will a gate be damaged while the wires above it are being etched?</td><td>metal area hanging on a gate against the gate's area, layer by layer</td></tr>
<tr><td>ERC (electrical rule check)</td><td>Is anything connected in an electrically unsafe way?</td><td>wells, taps, gates and supplies against connection rules</td></tr>
<tr><td>Reliability checks (Calibre PERC and similar)</td><td>Will it survive ESD, latch-up and voltage stress?</td><td>circuit topology and layout together: protection devices, resistances, spacings between voltage domains</td></tr>
<tr><td>Density and fill</td><td>Will polishing and etching be uniform?</td><td>the share of each layer inside moving windows</td></tr>
<tr><td>DFM (design for manufacturability)</td><td>Rules pass, but will it yield well?</td><td>recommended rules, via doubling, known bad patterns</td></tr>
</table></div>
<p>The levels of the job, roughly:</p>
<div class="tablewrap"><table>
<tr><th>Level</th><th>What you are trusted with</th></tr>
<tr><td>Runs checks</td><td>launches runs from a set-up someone else made, reports counts</td></tr>
<tr><td>Debugs</td><td>finds the root cause of any error in a block and tells the owner the exact fix</td></tr>
<tr><td>Owns a block</td><td>sets up the runs, keeps the waiver list, signs the block off</td></tr>
<tr><td>Owns the chip</td><td>top-level merge, fill, chip-level decks, tape-out checklist, foundry questions</td></tr>
<tr><td>Owns the flow</td><td>writes the automation, qualifies new deck versions, writes custom rules, trains others</td></tr>
</table></div>
<p>Service-company projects often keep people at the first two levels. Product companies hire for the last three. This roadmap is ordered to move you up that table.</p>

<p>Stages 1 to 7 add up to 30 weeks at five to seven hours a week. Stage 8 (the <a href="index.html">12-week scripting plan</a>) needs about seven hours a week of its own. Two ways to fit it in:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>One thing at a time:</strong> stages 1 to 7, then scripting. 42 weeks, about ten months, at five to seven hours a week.</li>
<li><strong>In parallel:</strong> start scripting when you start stage 3, because every topic from there gives you something to automate. About seven months in total, but twelve to fourteen hours a week during the twelve scripting weeks. Choose this only if you can really keep that up.</li>
</ul>
<div class="box tip"><strong>The one habit that matters most: an error diary.</strong> Every time you close a violation at work, write three lines in a private note: what the tool said, what the real cause was, how it was fixed. No design data, only the lesson. After six months this is your best interview preparation and the reason you debug faster than people with twice your years.</div>

<h2 id="s1">Stage 1 · Process, devices and layout basics</h2>
<p><strong>Why first.</strong> Every rule exists because of a step in the fab. If you know the step, the rule makes sense and you remember it; if you do not, the deck is 5,000 arbitrary numbers.</p>
<h4>What to learn</h4>
<ul>
<li>The CMOS process flow in order: wells, isolation, gate, source and drain implants, contacts, then the metal and via stack built layer by layer, then passivation and pads.</li>
<li>The cross-section of an NMOS and a PMOS, and of an inverter. Which drawn layers make which physical thing (active, poly, implants, contact, metal 1, via 1…).</li>
<li>Why wells need taps, what a parasitic thyristor is, and how it causes latch-up.</li>
<li>Lithography limits (why there is a minimum width and spacing), etching, polishing (why density matters), plasma charging (why antenna rules exist).</li>
<li>Standard cells: rows, rails, taps, fillers, endcaps, decaps, and why each physical-only cell exists.</li>
<li>Passive and special devices you will meet in LVS: resistors, capacitors, diodes, bipolar, varactors, ESD clamps.</li>
</ul>
<h4>How to practise</h4>
<ul>
<li>Draw the inverter cross-section from memory until you can do it in two minutes.</li>
<li>Open a standard cell in your layout viewer, turn layers on one at a time, and say what each shape becomes on silicon.</li>
<li>For ten rules in your foundry manual, write one sentence: "this rule exists because…".</li>
</ul>
<h4>Read and watch</h4>
<ul>
<li>Weste and Harris, <em>CMOS VLSI Design</em>, chapters 1 to 3 (introduction, MOS theory, fabrication and layout; chapter 3 has sections on design rule checking and circuit extraction). <a href="https://www.pearson.com/en-us/subject-catalog/p/cmos-vlsi-design-a-circuits-and-systems-perspective/P200000009477/9780321547743">Publisher page</a>.</li>
<li>Dan Clein, <em>CMOS IC Layout: Concepts, Methodologies, and Tools</em>: design rules, planning and review checklists from a layout engineer's side. <a href="https://shop.elsevier.com/books/cmos-ic-layout/clein/9780750671941">Publisher page</a>.</li>
<li>Christopher and Judy Saint, <em>IC Layout Basics</em>: a plain-English first book on processes and layout.</li>
<li>Adam Teman's recorded lecture courses from Bar-Ilan University, free on YouTube: the playlist <a href="https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLZU5hLL_713x0_AV_rVbay0pWmED7992G">Digital VLSI Design (RTL to GDS)</a>, and an introduction to integrated circuits course listed on his <a href="https://sites.biu.ac.il/en/adam-teman">university page</a>.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="s2">Stage 2 · Layout data and the sign-off flow</h2>
<h4>What to learn</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>Formats:</strong> GDSII and OASIS (cells, references, arrays, layers and datatypes, database units), LEF (abstracts and technology), DEF (placement and routing), SPICE/CDL and Verilog netlists.</li>
<li><strong>Hierarchy:</strong> why a cell placed 10,000 times is checked once, and what "flat count" and "hierarchical count" mean.</li>
<li><strong>What a PDK contains:</strong> design rule manual, layer map, technology files, rule decks for DRC, LVS, antenna, extraction and fill, device models, and the release notes that say which deck version to use.</li>
<li><strong>The hand-off:</strong> stream-out from place and route, merging cell and IP layouts, netlist export for LVS, conversion from Verilog to SPICE.</li>
<li><strong>The loop:</strong> run, triage, fix in the design tool, export, rerun; and where in-design checking fits.</li>
</ul>
<h4>How to practise</h4>
<ul>
<li>For one of your blocks, write down every input file of a DRC run and an LVS run, who produces it, and how you would know it is stale.</li>
<li>Work through the <a href="ch03_innovus.html#handoff">hand-off table</a> and find where each mistake would show up in your flow.</li>
<li>Read the release notes of your current deck version from start to end.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="s3">Stage 3 · DRC in depth</h2>
<h4>Rule families you must recognise on sight</h4>
<div class="tablewrap"><table>
<tr><th>Family</th><th>Meaning</th><th>Typical cause when it fails</th></tr>
<tr><td>Width</td><td>a shape is too narrow (or too wide)</td><td>hand edits, notches left by routing, wide-metal slotting rules</td></tr>
<tr><td>Spacing</td><td>two shapes on a layer are too close; often depends on width and parallel run length</td><td>congestion, macro and cell abutment, pin access</td></tr>
<tr><td>End-of-line, corner, jog</td><td>special spacing at line ends and corners</td><td>router patterns near pins and vias</td></tr>
<tr><td>Enclosure / extension</td><td>one layer must surround another by a margin (metal over via, implant over active)</td><td>via choice, cell abutment, custom IP</td></tr>
<tr><td>Minimum area and enclosed area</td><td>a shape or a hole is too small</td><td>short stubs, via landing pads, rings</td></tr>
<tr><td>Via rules</td><td>via size, spacing, arrays, stacking</td><td>power grid and ECO routing</td></tr>
<tr><td>Density</td><td>too little or too much of a layer inside a window</td><td>missing or blocked fill, empty areas over macros</td></tr>
<tr><td>Well, implant and tap rules</td><td>well spacing, tap distance, implant overlaps</td><td>missing tap cells, row ends, mixed-height cells</td></tr>
<tr><td>Off-grid and angle</td><td>shapes not on the manufacturing grid, or at forbidden angles</td><td>wrong units at stream-out, scaled or rotated IP</td></tr>
<tr><td>Voltage-dependent</td><td>spacing grows with the voltage difference between nets</td><td>high-voltage nets routed next to core nets, missing markers</td></tr>
</table></div>
<h4>What to learn</h4>
<ul>
<li>How to read the design rule manual: rule name, drawing, value, conditions, exceptions, and the "recommended" versus "mandatory" columns.</li>
<li>How to read a rule in the deck: enough SVRF to follow layer derivations (Boolean operations, sizing, selections) down to the final check.</li>
<li>Triage: real violation, export error, known waiver, or deck or switch problem. Triage comes before any fixing.</li>
<li>Run control: selecting rule groups, result limits, hierarchy, area-limited reruns, and why a clean partial run proves nothing.</li>
<li>Waivers: what may be waived, who approves, how it is documented, how you make sure a waiver does not hide new errors.</li>
<li>In-design checking in place and route versus sign-off, and why they disagree sometimes.</li>
</ul>
<h4>How to practise</h4>
<ul>
<li>For each family in the table, find one real violation in your error history and write the diary entry.</li>
<li>Pick five rules, follow each from the manual drawing to the deck code, and explain every derived layer.</li>
<li>In a free tool with an open-source process (see the <a href="#lab">home lab</a>), draw a small layout and break one rule of each family on purpose. Predict the error before you run.</li>
<li>Automate the daily numbers: scripts <a href="ch05_script_bank.html#p-summary">1</a>, <a href="ch05_script_bank.html#p-compare">3</a>, <a href="ch05_script_bank.html#p-waive">4</a> and <a href="ch05_script_bank.html#p-hotspot">12</a> of the script bank.</li>
</ul>
<h4>Read</h4>
<ul>
<li><a href="https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/calibre/2025/10/06/design-rule-checking-in-todays-integrated-circuit-design-environment">Design rule checking in today's IC design environment</a> (Siemens Calibre blog): rule growth, run time, and checking earlier.</li>
<li><a href="https://skywater-pdk.readthedocs.io/en/main/rules.html">SkyWater SKY130 design rules</a>: a public rule manual for a real 130 nm process, with sections for layers and device recognition, periphery rules, antenna rules and high-voltage methodology. Open PDKs like this one are the way to study a real manual without a confidentiality agreement.</li>
<li><a href="https://www.klayout.de/doc/manual/drc.html">KLayout DRC manual</a>: how a rule deck is built from layer operations, in a free tool.</li>
<li>Your foundry's design rule manual and the SVRF manual. Nothing replaces them.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="s4">Stage 4 · LVS in depth</h2>
<h4>What LVS does, in three steps</h4>
<ol>
<li><strong>Extract:</strong> recognise devices from layer combinations (poly over active is a transistor), find which shapes are connected (through contacts and vias), and attach names from text labels. The result is a layout netlist.</li>
<li><strong>Compare:</strong> match the layout netlist against the source netlist, cell by cell where the hierarchy corresponds, using ports and names as starting points.</li>
<li><strong>Report:</strong> what could not be matched: nets, instances, ports, and device properties.</li>
</ol>
<h4>Error types and the order to attack them</h4>
<div class="tablewrap"><table>
<tr><th>Order</th><th>Error</th><th>What it looks like</th><th>First things to check</th></tr>
<tr><td>1</td><td>Extraction and input problems</td><td>missing cells, text warnings, wrong top</td><td>export, black-box list, text layers, case sensitivity</td></tr>
<tr><td>2</td><td>Power and ground shorts</td><td>far fewer nets in layout; huge mismatch everywhere</td><td>isolate with the tool's short-isolation feature; look at straps, well ties, fill, labels</td></tr>
<tr><td>3</td><td>Other shorts</td><td>two source nets match one layout net</td><td>overlapping wires, wrong via, a label on the wrong shape</td></tr>
<tr><td>4</td><td>Opens</td><td>one source net matches two layout nets</td><td>missing via, broken route, pin not reached, net named by text on one piece only</td></tr>
<tr><td>5</td><td>Port errors</td><td>missing or extra ports at top level</td><td>pin text missing, wrong layer or wrong hierarchy level</td></tr>
<tr><td>6</td><td>Instance and device mismatches</td><td>missing or extra devices or cells</td><td>physical-only cells in the netlist, wrong cell version, filtered devices</td></tr>
<tr><td>7</td><td>Property errors</td><td>sizes differ (width, length, fingers, resistor value)</td><td>cell version, parameter mapping, tolerance settings</td></tr>
</table></div>
<p>Always work from the top of this table down and from the lowest cell in the hierarchy up. One short at the bottom creates thousands of messages above it.</p>
<h4>What to learn</h4>
<ul>
<li>Device recognition and how the deck decides terminals (gate, source, drain, bulk).</li>
<li>Text: which layers carry labels, top-level versus lower-level text, the virtual connect options and why they are dangerous at sign-off.</li>
<li>Hierarchy: corresponding cells, when the tool flattens a cell, black boxes for hard IP and what they stop checking.</li>
<li>Soft connections: two points joined only through a well or substrate. Electrically weak, and a classic late surprise.</li>
<li>Reduction: how parallel and series devices and filler-type devices are merged or filtered before comparison.</li>
<li>Reading the LVS report section by section (<a href="ch04_calibre.html#lvs">Calibre page</a>) and cross-probing in the results viewer.</li>
</ul>
<h4>How to practise</h4>
<ul>
<li>In the home lab, take a clean small design and inject one error at a time: delete a via, swap two labels, short two wires, remove a pin text, change a transistor width. Run LVS and study exactly how each one is reported. This single exercise teaches more than a year of fixing other people's errors.</li>
<li>Automate the first look: scripts <a href="ch05_script_bank.html#p-lvs">9</a> and <a href="ch05_script_bank.html#p-cdl">8</a>.</li>
</ul>
<h4>Read</h4>
<ul>
<li><a href="https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/calibre/2022/08/03/lvs-zero-to-hero-in-3-easy-steps/">LVS zero to hero in 3 easy steps</a> (Siemens Calibre blog): short isolation, hierarchy cells, when to black-box, device-level debug, with links to videos.</li>
<li><a href="https://www.electronicdesign.com/markets/automation/article/21123526/a-guide-on-lvs-in-the-nanometer-era">A guide on LVS in the nanometer era</a> (Electronic Design): the extract-then-compare flow and debugging cases.</li>
<li><a href="https://klayout.de/doc/manual/lvs_intro.html">KLayout LVS introduction</a>: the same concepts in a free tool, including how extraction is scripted.</li>
<li><a href="http://opencircuitdesign.com/netgen/">Netgen</a>: the open-source LVS comparison tool used with open processes.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="s5">Stage 5 · Antenna, ERC, reliability, density and DFM</h2>
<h3>Antenna</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Physics:</strong> during plasma etching, a metal piece that is connected to a gate but not yet to any diffusion collects charge. If the metal is large compared with the gate, the voltage can damage the thin gate oxide.</li>
<li><strong>The rule:</strong> a maximum ratio of collecting metal (and via) area to gate area, per layer, sometimes accumulated over layers; a connection to diffusion raises the allowed ratio because it lets charge leak away.</li>
<li><strong>Fixes:</strong> jump to a higher metal near the gate so the long part is not connected to the gate until later ("layer hopping"); add an antenna diode next to the gate; break a long route with a buffer.</li>
<li><strong>Know:</strong> why a net can pass at block level and fail at top (the top route adds metal), and why diodes added for antenna need an LVS rerun.</li>
<li>Read: <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_effect">Antenna effect</a> (Wikipedia) for the physics and the usual rule form; the antenna section of the <a href="https://skywater-pdk.readthedocs.io/en/main/rules.html">SKY130 rules</a> for a real rule set.</li>
</ul>
<h3>ERC</h3>
<ul>
<li>Floating gates and floating wells; wells tied to the wrong supply; forward-biased junctions.</li>
<li>Well and substrate taps: presence and maximum distance, the layout side of latch-up prevention.</li>
<li>Soft-connect checks; devices with gate tied directly to a supply where the process forbids it.</li>
<li>Know which ERC checks run inside your LVS deck and which are separate runs.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Reliability checks (Calibre PERC and equivalents)</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>ESD:</strong> every pad has a protection path; clamp devices exist and are sized; the resistance from pad to clamp and the current density in that path are within limits.</li>
<li><strong>Latch-up:</strong> guard rings exist, are wide enough, connected, and close enough to the devices that inject current.</li>
<li><strong>Voltage domains:</strong> signals crossing between supplies go through level shifters; thin-oxide devices never see high voltage; spacing between nets depends on their voltage difference.</li>
<li>Why these need both netlist topology and layout geometry at once, which neither DRC nor LVS has alone.</li>
<li>Read: <a href="https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/calibre/2018/01/09/tiny-chips-big-challenges-full-chip-electrical-reliability/">Full-chip electrical reliability</a> and <a href="https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/calibre/2025/09/23/safeguarding-ic-reliability-calibre-percs-latch-up-guard-ring-check/">latch-up guard ring checks</a> (Siemens Calibre blog); the high-voltage methodology section of the <a href="https://skywater-pdk.readthedocs.io/en/main/rules.html">SKY130 rules</a> for voltage-dependent rules in a real manual.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Density, fill and DFM</h3>
<ul>
<li>Why uneven density makes polishing uneven (dishing and erosion) and changes wire thickness.</li>
<li>Minimum and maximum density per window, density gradient between neighbouring windows.</li>
<li>Fill: metal, via, poly and active fill; fill keep-out near sensitive analog and RF; why fill changes timing and must be included in extraction; fill is redone after every ECO.</li>
<li>DFM: recommended rules, doubled vias, wire spreading, lithography hotspot patterns.</li>
</ul>
<h4>How to practise this stage</h4>
<ul>
<li>For one antenna violation, compute the ratio by hand from the layout and compare with the report.</li>
<li>List every ERC and reliability check your project runs, with one line on what each protects against.</li>
<li>Find the density windows of one block that are closest to the limit and explain why.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="s6">Stage 6 · Advanced nodes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Multi-patterning:</strong> when features are closer than one exposure can print, a layer is split across two or more masks ("colours"). Shapes closer than the same-mask spacing must be on different masks. A ring of an odd number of such neighbours cannot be coloured, and that is the classic error. Colouring is closer to a routing problem with many legal answers than to a pass or fail rule.</li>
<li><strong>FinFET and later devices:</strong> fins and gates on fixed grids, transistor widths only in whole fins, one routing direction per metal layer, cut shapes, many more end-of-line and context-dependent rules.</li>
<li><strong>EUV:</strong> one exposure replaces several masks on some layers and brings its own rules.</li>
<li><strong>Scale:</strong> rule counts and run times grow sharply, so hierarchy handling, distributed runs and in-design checking become part of the job.</li>
<li><strong>Layout-dependent effects:</strong> the neighbourhood of a transistor changes its behaviour, so extraction needs more layout context.</li>
</ul>
<h4>Read</h4>
<ul>
<li><a href="https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/calibre/2015/01/28/a-look-behind-the-mask-of-multi-patterning/">A look behind the mask of multi-patterning</a> (Siemens Calibre blog) and the paper <a href="https://resources.sw.siemens.com/en-US/white-paper-assume-nothing-clearing-up-common-misconceptions-about-multi-patterning">Assume nothing: common misconceptions about multi-patterning</a> (free with registration).</li>
<li><a href="https://semiengineering.com/brave-world-finfets">Brave new world of FinFETs</a> (Semiconductor Engineering): what changed for layout.</li>
</ul>
<p>If your current project is on a mature node, this stage is still worth three weeks: product-company interviews ask about it, and the concepts explain the direction of every newer deck.</p>

<h2 id="s7">Stage 7 · Chip level and tape-out</h2>
<h4>What changes from block to chip</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>Merge:</strong> blocks, hard IP, memories, IO ring, and the chip frame are combined. Cell name clashes, different database units and missing IP layouts appear here.</li>
<li><strong>Chip-only structures:</strong> seal ring, scribe-line and corner structures, pad openings, redistribution layers and bumps, logos and labels the foundry requires.</li>
<li><strong>Chip-level deck switches:</strong> full-chip density, chip corner rules, seal-ring rules, final-metal and pad rules that are skipped at block level.</li>
<li><strong>Top-level LVS:</strong> pads and bumps as ports, black-boxed IP with trusted abstracts, power domains.</li>
<li><strong>Final fill</strong> and a final DRC after fill.</li>
<li><strong>Waivers and IP:</strong> the waiver package for the foundry; proof that each black-boxed IP was itself signed off with the right deck version.</li>
<li><strong>Run time:</strong> full-chip runs take hours to days, so you plan them: partial reruns, rule grouping, machine and licence planning, and results that survive a crash.</li>
<li><strong>The checklist:</strong> the foundry's tape-out form, deck versions, layer usage, and the final data check before the file leaves the company.</li>
</ul>
<h4>How to practise</h4>
<ul>
<li>Ask to shadow your project's tape-out checklist review. Read the checklist beforehand and write a question for each line you do not understand.</li>
<li>Diff the block-level and chip-level run set-ups of your project and explain every difference.</li>
<li>Build the "pre-run gate" and "nightly dashboard" projects from the <a href="ch05_script_bank.html#next">script bank</a>; chip leads notice the person who brings these.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="s9">Stage 9 · Towards senior</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Rule writing:</strong> write small SVRF checks of your own for debug and for project-specific rules, then read TVF.</li>
<li><strong>Deck qualification:</strong> when a new deck version arrives, compare results on known designs and explain every difference before the project switches.</li>
<li><strong>A second tool:</strong> Synopsys <a href="https://synopsys.com/icvalidator">IC Validator</a> and Cadence Pegasus do the same jobs with different languages. Knowing the concepts makes the second tool a matter of weeks, and product companies value people who are not tied to one.</li>
<li><strong>Neighbouring skills:</strong> parasitic extraction inputs, basic place and route, IO and ESD basics, packaging and bumps.</li>
<li><strong>People skills:</strong> clear violation reports for block owners, saying "this is not clean" under schedule pressure, teaching juniors.</li>
</ul>

<h4>Ten lab exercises</h4>
<ol>
<li>Install KLayout, open a standard cell from the open PDK, identify every layer.</li>
<li>Draw two metal lines and move them together until the spacing rule fails. Repeat for width, enclosure and minimum area.</li>
<li>Read five rules in the SKY130 manual and reproduce each violation.</li>
<li>Extract an inverter and compare it with a hand-written netlist.</li>
<li>Inject, one at a time: an open, a short, a swapped label, a missing pin, a wrong width. Record how each is reported.</li>
<li>Remove a well tap and find which check catches it.</li>
<li>Build a long metal line to a gate and trigger the antenna rule; fix it by layer hop, then by diode.</li>
<li>Run a small design through the open flow and run DRC and LVS on the result.</li>
<li>Point your summary and comparison scripts at the open tools' reports, adjusting the patterns.</li>
<li>Write a new, simple rule in a KLayout DRC script and test it on a layout that should pass and one that should fail.</li>
</ol>
<p>The free tools differ from Calibre in commands and report formats. The physics, the rule families and the debugging logic are identical, and those are what interviews test.</p>

<h4>What the postings ask for</h4>
<p>Qualcomm physical verification postings found while writing this page (they vary by team and level, and most copies are on job-aggregator sites) ask for: block and full-chip sign-off with a deep understanding of DRC, LVS, ERC, reliability (PERC), antenna and density checks; hands-on place-and-route tool experience (Innovus, ICC2 or Fusion Compiler); and scripting in Tcl, Python or Perl, required in some roles and a plus in others. The deck-development roles add writing and debugging Calibre or IC Validator rule files and linking LVS to extraction tools. One example: a <a href="https://qualcomm.dejobs.org/bangalore-ind/physical-verification-engineer/259080EAE77547C1B826C53CB49032EA/job/">Physical Verification Engineer posting, Bangalore</a> (it may have closed; the company careers site has the current ones). That list is stages 3 to 9 of this page.</p>
<p>Concept question lists to test yourself: the <a href="https://physical-design-interview-guide.github.io/categories/physical-verification/">physical design interview guide's PV section</a> and <a href="https://resources.skill-lync.com/branches/electronics-communication/questions/vlsi-design/physical-verification-signoff">Skill-Lync's sign-off questions</a>. I could not confirm any public question list specific to one company, so check recent interview reports on Glassdoor and ask the recruiter what each round covers.</p>

<h2 id="ready">Ready-for-a-product-company checklist</h2>
<ul class="check">
<li>I can draw an inverter cross-section and explain latch-up and the antenna effect on a whiteboard.</li>
<li>Given any DRC violation picture, I can name the rule family and two likely causes.</li>
<li>I can read a rule from the manual and follow it through the deck.</li>
<li>I debug LVS in a fixed order and can explain why that order.</li>
<li>I can explain what changes between block and chip sign-off.</li>
<li>I can explain multi-patterning and FinFET layout restrictions in simple words.</li>
<li>I have signed off at least one block myself, including its waiver list.</li>
<li>My team uses at least one script I wrote, and I can describe it in two minutes.</li>
<li>I have an error diary with fifty or more entries.</li>
<li>I have done the injected-error LVS lab and the rule-breaking DRC lab at home.</li>
</ul>
